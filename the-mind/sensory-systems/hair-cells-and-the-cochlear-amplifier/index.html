Hair Cells and the Cochlear Amplifier

What it is. Hair cells turn vibration into electrical signals. Inner hair cells report to the brain; outer hair cells change length with each vibration and amplify the motion of the cochlea.

What it gives you. Hair cells do not regrow in mammals, so their loss is the main cause of permanent hearing loss. Their activity even produces faint sounds that are used to screen newborns.

A wave along a coiled strip

Inside the cochlea, sound sets up a travelling wave along the basilar membrane. The base is narrow and stiff and responds to high pitches; the far end is wide and heavy and responds to low pitches. The coil only saves space.

The first measurements were made in cadaver ears at very loud levels and showed broad tuning. In living ears, measured with lasers, each point is far more sharply tuned at quiet levels. Something alive was sharpening the response.

That something is the outer hair cell. It shortens and lengthens in time with the sound, adding force at the right moment. This cochlear amplifier adds roughly 40 to 60 dB of sensitivity and sharpens tuning.

From movement to signal

  1. Sound moves the basilar membrane and bends the bundle of stereocilia on each hair cell.
  2. Fine tip links pull open channels at the tops of the stereocilia.
  3. Potassium flows in from the surrounding fluid and the cell becomes charged.
  4. Inner hair cells release glutamate onto auditory nerve fibres.
  5. Outer hair cells change length and push the membrane, amplifying the motion.

The hairs are stiff rods packed with actin. They pivot at the base like poles in a storm.

Inner and outer hair cells

  • Inner hair cells One row, about 3,500 per ear. About 95% of nerve fibres carrying sound to the brain contact them.
  • Outer hair cells Three rows, about 12,000 per ear. Few fibres report from them; they act as the amplifier.
  • Teamwork Without outer hair cells, sensitivity drops 40 to 60 dB and tuning becomes broad.

What hair cells mean for health

  • No regrowth Mammals never replace lost hair cells. Supporting cells form scars.
  • Birds regrow Birds replace hair cells within weeks, which drives research into repair.
  • Causes of loss Loud noise, some antibiotics, meningitis and ageing.
  • Newborn screening A click in the ear canal evokes an echo from healthy outer hair cells.
  • Tinnitus Ringing with no sound in the ear. It arises in the brain, like a phantom limb.
  • No cancer Inner ear cells do not divide, so tumours do not arise from them.

The cochlea

  • 3,500 inner hair cells per ear
  • 12,000 outer hair cells per ear
  • 40-140 stereocilia per hair cell
  • 40-60 dB gain of the cochlear amplifier
  • nanometres size of the movements that are heard
  • 1961 the one Nobel Prize given for hearing research

Isolated outer hair cells, driven by an electrical copy of music, visibly stretch and shrink in time with it. No other cell in the body moves like this at sound frequencies.

Safety. Keep any test sound at a comfortable level, and stop at once if your ears ring or hurt.

Steps

  1. Sit in a very quiet room for two minutes.
  2. Listen closely to the silence.
  3. Many people notice a faint hum or ring. Some ears also give out a faint sound, measurable with a microphone.
  4. Hum a low note, then a high note, with a hand on your throat and the other on your head.
  5. Low notes vibrate the far end of the cochlea; high notes the near end. Each spot along it has its own best frequency.

Time. 3 minutes

Words to know

Basilar membrane
The flexible strip running along the cochlea on which the hair cells sit.
Stereocilia
The stiff rods on top of a hair cell. Bending them opens channels.
Prestin
The motor protein that makes outer hair cells change length.
Otoacoustic emission
A faint sound produced by the inner ear, recorded in the ear canal.
