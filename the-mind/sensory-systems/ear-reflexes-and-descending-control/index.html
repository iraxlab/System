Ear Reflexes and Descending Control

What it is. The brain sends signals back down to the ear. Two reflexes, the olivocochlear system and the middle-ear muscles, turn hearing down to protect it, reduce masking by noise and support attention.

What it gives you. They explain why ringing ears after a loud event are a warning, why we hear speech in a noisy restaurant, and why your own voice does not deafen you.

The brain turns down the ear

Alongside every upward station of the hearing pathway runs a downward one. The best understood ends in the ear itself: neurons in the superior olive send fibres to the outer hair cells and to the nerve endings under the inner hair cells.

Their transmitter, acetylcholine, makes outer hair cells move less, lowering the cochlear amplifier by about 25 dB. The reflex is fast, under 5 milliseconds, and sound in either ear triggers it, much as light in one eye narrows both pupils.

A second reflex uses two tiny middle-ear muscles. They stiffen the chain of bones and cut low-pitched sound by up to about 25 dB. Both reflexes protect the ear and help us hear signals in noise.

Four jobs

  • Protection With the fibres cut on one side, a loud noise damages that ear far more.
  • Range shift It shifts the nerve's working range up by about 20 dB, so loud backgrounds do not saturate it.
  • Less masking Noise uses up transmitter at the hair cell synapse. Turning noise down saves it for the signal.
  • Attention Animals attending to a light task showed smaller ear responses to irrelevant clicks.
  • Band by band Fibres are tuned and arranged by frequency, so hearing can be turned down in chosen bands.
  • Too fast for thought The reflex runs through the brainstem, not the cortex.

Stapedius and tensor tympani

  • Stapedius Pulls on the stirrup. Controlled by the facial nerve.
  • Tensor tympani Pulls on the hammer and tenses the eardrum. Controlled by the trigeminal nerve.
  • Low pitches Contraction mainly cuts low frequencies, reducing masking by rumble.

Factory workers with a one-sided facial palsy showed temporary hearing loss only in the palsied ear after a loud shift.

From sound back to the ear

  1. Sound excites the auditory nerve.
  2. The nerve drives cells in the cochlear nucleus.
  3. These cells drive olivocochlear neurons in the superior olive, on both sides.
  4. Their fibres release acetylcholine onto outer hair cells.
  5. The outer hair cells move less and the ear becomes less sensitive, within milliseconds.

Ringing after loud sound means the ear was damaged. The reflexes help, but they cannot protect against everything.

Reflexes and noise

  • 136 dB measured in a loud sports stadium
  • 110 dB possible on a subway platform
  • 25 dB reduction by the olivocochlear system
  • 25 dB low-frequency reduction by the middle-ear muscles
  • 5 ms reflex delay, too short for a cortical loop
  • 2 : 1 ratio of olivocochlear neurons driven by the same versus the other ear

Bats calling at about 120 dB contract their middle-ear muscles just before each call, then relax them in time to hear the faint echo.

Safety. Keep any test sound at a comfortable level, and stop at once if your ears ring or hurt.

Steps

  1. Turn on a car radio at low volume on a quiet street, then on a busy road.
  2. On the road you turn it up. Low road rumble masks the music, and your middle-ear muscles also tighten in loud noise.
  3. Read something absorbing while music plays in the next room.
  4. The music seems to fade. The brain can turn down the ear while you attend to vision.
  5. Speak aloud and notice how loud your voice feels.
  6. Middle-ear muscles contract just before and during speech, so your own voice is softened.

Time. 3 minutes

Words to know

Olivocochlear system
Nerve fibres from the superior olive in the brainstem to the hair cells of the cochlea.
Stapedius
The smallest muscle in the body. It pulls on the stirrup bone to damp sound.
Masking
One sound making another harder or impossible to hear.
Consensual reflex
A reflex triggered from either side that acts on both sides, like the pupils.
