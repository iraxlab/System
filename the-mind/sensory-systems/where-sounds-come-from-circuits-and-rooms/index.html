Where Sounds Come From: Circuits and Rooms

What it is. Level differences are computed by a simple balance of excitation and inhibition in the lateral superior olive. Time and level cues meet in the inferior colliculus, and the first part of a sound sets its perceived place in a room full of echoes.

What it gives you. It explains how we locate a speaker in a reverberant hall, why music halls and speech theatres are built differently, and how experience reshapes the brain's map of space.

Balancing two ears

In the lateral superior olive, sound in the same-side ear excites each cell. Sound in the other ear arrives through an extra relay that releases glycine and inhibits it. The louder side wins, so the cell's firing reports the level difference.

This nucleus gives most of its space to high frequencies, where level differences are large. The time comparator gives most of its space to low frequencies. Each nucleus favours the range where its cue works.

Both feed the inferior colliculus, where single cells first respond strongly to both cues. Losing one colliculus leaves an animal unable to locate sounds on the opposite side.

Time against level

  • In headphones A time cue pointing left and a level cue pointing right can cancel, and the sound seems straight ahead.
  • Balance More of one cue moves the image to its side.
  • Where Cells in the inferior colliculus respond to both cues together and are the likely basis.

The precedence effect

  1. In a room, reflections reach the ears from every wall, scrambling the time cue moment by moment.
  2. Yet listeners point to the true source, because the first few milliseconds decide direction.
  3. Delays up to about 0.6 ms pull the image toward the earlier speaker.
  4. From about 0.6 to 10 ms, one sound is heard at the first speaker, but the room sounds fuller.
  5. Beyond that, a distinct echo is heard.

Played backwards, a hammer striking a brick in a cathedral reveals a long swell of echoes we normally never notice.

How long rooms ring

  • 0 s open air
  • 0.4 s living room
  • 0.9 s theatre built for speech
  • 1.3 s opera house
  • 2.4 s symphony hall
  • 13 s large cathedral

A specialist in hearing

  • Hunting in darkness It catches a mouse by sound alone. Its eyes are fixed, so it turns its whole head.
  • Facial ruff Feathers on the face act as an outer ear. Its flight feathers are silent.
  • Clean timing circuit Its time comparator matches the 1948 delay-line model closely.
  • A true space map Neurons in its midbrain are laid out by direction, mostly below and to the opposite side.
  • Prism experiment Young owls wearing prisms shifted their sound map to match the shifted visual world.
  • Lasting trace Adults cannot shift, unless they wore prisms when young. New axon growth is the likely cause.

Mammals show no space map in the main hearing pathway. The one exception is in the deep layers of the superior colliculus, where sound and sight maps line up.

Safety. Keep any test sound at a comfortable level, and stop at once if your ears ring or hurt.

Steps

  1. Stand in a tiled bathroom or stairwell and clap once.
  2. You hear one clap from your hands, although echoes arrive from every wall.
  3. Ask a friend to speak while walking round you in that room, your eyes closed.
  4. You still track them. The brain uses the first few milliseconds of each sound and suppresses later echoes.
  5. Go outdoors to an open field and clap again.
  6. The clap sounds dry and short. Outdoors there are no walls, so there is no reverberation.

Time. 5 minutes

Words to know

Precedence effect
The first-arriving sound sets the perceived direction; later echoes are suppressed.
Reverberation time
How long sound takes to die away in a room.
Glycine
An inhibitory transmitter, used here to subtract one ear from the other.
Space map
An orderly layout of neurons by the direction of the sound they respond to.
