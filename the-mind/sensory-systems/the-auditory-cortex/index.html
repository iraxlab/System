The Auditory Cortex

What it is. The auditory cortex holds several frequency maps arranged in columns. It reorganises after hearing loss, is needed to locate sounds, and contains a region that responds to clear musical pitch.

What it gives you. It explains why the brain adapts to age-related hearing loss, and why pitch stays the same on a cheap speaker that cannot play the bass.

Maps that can change

The auditory cortex lies in the temporal lobe. In humans, primary cortex sits on Heschl's gyrus, hidden on the upper surface of the temporal lobe. Several fields each map frequency, and neighbouring maps meet in mirror image, as in vision.

A recording electrode going straight down through the six layers finds the same best frequency throughout: frequency is organised in columns. Many cortical cells also prefer a particular loudness and fall silent when the sound gets louder.

These maps were long thought fixed. Then experiments showed that after a small high-frequency hearing loss, the silent stretch of cortex takes up the neighbouring frequencies within weeks. The reorganisation grows larger at each stage up the pathway.

After a small hearing loss

  1. A small part of the cochlea is damaged, removing hearing at 10 to 20 kHz.
  2. At first, the matching cortex simply falls silent.
  3. After about three weeks, it responds to the edge frequencies instead.
  4. Possible causes: new axon growth, or weak existing inputs being strengthened.
  5. The same is likely in age-related hearing loss, which develops over decades.

Whether the enlarged areas improve hearing at those frequencies is still open.

The cortex is needed

  • One side lost Removing A1 on one side leaves an animal unable to locate sounds on the opposite side.
  • Band by band Removing only part of the map impairs localisation only for the matching frequencies.
  • Several fields Reversible cooling shows A1 and two neighbouring fields are needed; others are not.

Each field likely does many things. Localisation is simply one that is well tested.

What makes a clear pitch

  • Harmonics Musical notes contain a fundamental and its exact multiples.
  • Invariance Pitch stays the same across instruments, loudness and direction.
  • Missing fundamental Remove the lowest component and the pitch stays, because the pattern still repeats at the same rate.
  • A pitch region Brain scans show an area beside Heschl's gyrus active for clear pitch and quiet for weak pitch.
  • Lower stations The brainstem and midbrain respond equally to strong and weak pitch.
  • Implants Electrodes are ranked by pitch when an implant is first set up.

The hearing cortex

  • 4-5 frequency maps found in the human auditory cortex
  • 6 layers, with the same best frequency in a column
  • 3 weeks+ before the cortex reorganises after hearing loss
  • 5 degrees location difference a cat finds correctly 75% of the time
  • 10-12 degrees with only one working ear
  • 50-100 ms test sounds, too brief to move the head

Brain scanners are very loud. Hearing studies use earmuffs, sound piped in through tubes, and images timed to the heartbeat, because each pulse moves the brainstem slightly.

Safety. Keep any test sound at a comfortable level, and stop at once if your ears ring or hurt.

Steps

  1. Play a song through a tiny phone speaker, then through good headphones, at a comfortable volume.
  2. The tune and the bass line keep the same pitch, though the small speaker produces almost no low frequencies.
  3. Listen to the same note on two instruments, such as piano and guitar.
  4. Same pitch, different quality. Pitch comes from the repeating pattern of the harmonics; quality from their balance.

Time. 3 minutes

Words to know

A1
Primary auditory cortex, the main frequency-mapped area of the hearing cortex.
Heschl's gyrus
A small ridge on the upper surface of the temporal lobe where A1 lies in humans.
Missing fundamental
Hearing the pitch of a note even when its lowest frequency is absent.
Presbycusis
Hearing loss with age, mainly at high frequencies.
