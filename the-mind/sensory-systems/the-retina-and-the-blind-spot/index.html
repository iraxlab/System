The Retina and the Blind Spot

What it is. The retina is a thin sheet of neurons at the back of the eye. Rods and cones catch light, and two main kinds of ganglion cell send a compressed report to the brain.

What it gives you. The design of the retina explains sharp central vision, poor night vision in the centre, the blind spot, and why our eyes face forward.

A sensor built inside out

Light passes through several layers of neurons before it reaches the rods and cones at the back of the retina. The signal then travels forward again, through bipolar cells to ganglion cells, whose axons leave the eye together as the optic nerve.

Where those axons exit there is no room for receptors. That patch is the blind spot. You never notice it, because the brain fills it in and because the other eye sees what it misses.

Animals with eyes on the side of the head see almost all round. Primates gave up the rear view for a large overlap between the two eyes, which gives fine depth. To make that work, fibres from the inner half of each retina cross to the other side of the brain.

One human retina

  • 120 million rods, for dim light
  • 6 million cones, for daylight and colour
  • 200,000 cones per square millimetre in the fovea
  • 1 in 8 cones is a blue cone
  • 1 degree of view is about a thumbnail at arm's length
  • 1 million ganglion cells report everything to the brain

Rods and cones

  • Rods Very sensitive, used at night, colour-blind. Absent from the fovea, so the centre of vision is poor in the dark.
  • Cones Work in daylight, give sharp vision and colour. Packed most densely in the fovea.
  • Light makes them quieter In darkness receptors release transmitter steadily. Light reduces that release.

From light to optic nerve

  1. Rods and cones catch light at the back of the retina.
  2. Horizontal cells link neighbouring receptors and create the surround of each receptive field.
  3. Bipolar cells carry the signal inward. Half respond to light, half to dark.
  4. Amacrine cells, more than 20 kinds, shape timing and handle the rod signal.
  5. Ganglion cells fire spikes and send the result to the brain through the optic nerve.

Receptors, horizontal and bipolar cells use graded voltages. Only the last stage fires spikes.

Two main ganglion cell types

  • Midget cells Small fields fed by a single cone in the centre of the retina. They signal fine detail and colour, with a sustained response.
  • Parasol cells Fields about three times wider, fed by mixed cones. They signal motion and change, with a brief burst.
  • Centre and surround Most cells respond to a small spot in the centre and are inhibited by light around it, so they report contrast.

The image on the retina is upside down. In one experiment, words shown to a mouse could be read, inverted, in the pattern of activated cells on its flattened retina.

Steps

  1. On paper, draw a small cross on the left and a dot 8 cm to the right.
  2. Hold the paper at arm's length. Close your left eye.
  3. Look only at the cross with your right eye and slowly bring the paper closer.
  4. At one distance the dot disappears and the paper looks whole. The brain filled the gap where the optic nerve leaves the eye.
  5. Open both eyes and repeat.
  6. The dot stays visible. The two blind spots fall on different parts of the scene, so each eye covers for the other.
  7. At night, look slightly beside a faint star instead of straight at it.
  8. It looks brighter off-centre. The very centre of the retina has cones only and no rods.

Time. 5 minutes

Words to know

Fovea
The small central pit of the retina, packed with cones and used for sharp vision.
Ganglion cell
The output cell of the retina. Its axons form the optic nerve.
Optic chiasm
The crossing point under the brain where fibres from the inner half of each retina switch sides.
Filling in
The brain supplying what belongs in a gap so the picture looks whole.
