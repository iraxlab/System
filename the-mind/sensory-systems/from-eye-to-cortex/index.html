From Eye to Cortex

What it is. The thalamus relays each eye's signal in separate layers to the primary visual cortex, where cells start to respond to edges, their orientation, direction of motion and the input of both eyes.

What it gives you. This is where the visual image is first taken apart into features. The same principles of columns and maps appear across the cortex.

The image taken apart

The optic nerve ends in the lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus. It has six layers. Each layer receives one eye only, and the cells there respond much as retinal cells do, with a centre and an opposite surround.

In the primary visual cortex the picture changes. Cells respond best to edges at one angle, many only to motion in one direction, and most above the input layer combine the two eyes. A map of the visual field covers the whole area.

The centre of gaze takes far more cortex than the edge of vision, because cortex is the same thickness everywhere and the fovea has the most receptors. Beyond V1 lie more than 30 further visual areas, richly linked to each other.

Six layers in the thalamus

  • Four upper layers Small cells that receive midget ganglion cells: fine detail and colour.
  • Two lower layers Large cells that receive parasol ganglion cells: motion and change.
  • One eye per layer Layers alternate between the two eyes. The left thalamus sees the right half of the world.

What V1 adds

  • Orientation Cells prefer edges at one angle. This was found by chance, when the edge of a projector slide made a cell fire.
  • Direction Many cells fire for movement one way and stay quiet for the reverse.
  • Spatial frequency Cells prefer stripes of a certain fineness, neither too coarse nor too fine.
  • Two eyes Above the input layer, most cells respond to both eyes, which prepares depth.
  • Simple cells Separate zones for light and for dark, side by side.
  • Complex cells Larger fields that respond to light and dark edges anywhere inside them.

Columns and maps

  • Eye stripes Input from each eye forms alternating stripes, finer than the ridges of a fingerprint.
  • Orientation pinwheels Optical imaging shows preferred angles swirling around central points, messier than early tidy models.
  • Mirror maps Where one visual area meets the next, the map of the visual field reverses like a mirror.

A good model is a tool, not a belief. Several neat diagrams of V1 were dropped once better images arrived.

A few of the higher areas

  • V2 Receptive fields about three times larger than in V1, organised in thick, thin and pale stripes.
  • V4 Large, complex fields that change with attention and eye movements. Not only a colour area.
  • MT Almost every cell is tuned to direction of motion. Fed mainly by the parasol system.

Each area handles several kinds of information. The idea of one area per feature has not held up.

Early researchers shone diffuse light into the eye and found no response in the visual cortex. The cells were there all along; they simply ignore even light and wait for an edge.

Steps

  1. Hold a pencil upright at arm's length and look at it.
  2. Tilt it slowly through a full half-turn.
  3. Each angle drives a different group of cells in the primary visual cortex. You notice even a small tilt.
  4. Close one eye, then the other, while keeping the pencil still.
  5. It appears to jump. Each eye has its own stripes of input in the cortex, merged in the layers above.

Time. 3 minutes

Words to know

LGN
Lateral geniculate nucleus, the visual relay in the thalamus.
V1
Primary visual cortex at the back of the brain, the first cortical stage of vision.
Orientation selectivity
A cell firing most for an edge at one particular angle.
Ocular dominance column
A stripe of cortex driven mainly by one eye.
