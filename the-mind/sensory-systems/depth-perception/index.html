Depth Perception

What it is. The retina is flat, so the brain rebuilds depth from several cues: the difference between the two eyes, relative motion, shading, perspective, size and overlap.

What it gives you. Depth guides every reach, step and catch. Knowing the cues explains why people without stereo vision can still drive, and why threading a needle is hard with one eye.

Three dimensions from two flat images

Because the eyes sit apart, each sees a slightly different view. Cells in the visual cortex compare the two and respond to particular differences: some to objects at the point of focus, some nearer, some farther.

A handful of broadly tuned disparity channels, perhaps four to six, are enough to compute fine depth, much as three cone types are enough for colour. A shift of a single pixel on a screen can be seen as depth.

Stereo vision is only one route. Motion parallax, shading, perspective and overlap all add depth. When cues agree, depth is seen faster and more surely; when they conflict, the picture becomes unstable.

How depth is rebuilt

  • Stereopsis Disparity between the two eyes. Best at close range.
  • Motion parallax Relative motion as you move. Works at long distances.
  • Shading Light has come from above for millions of years. A disc lit on top looks like a bump.
  • Perspective Parallel lines converge and objects shrink with distance.
  • Overlap An object that hides part of another is nearer.
  • Eye muscles Focusing and turning the eyes inward give a rough sense of distance.

When the two eyes never learned to work together

  • Who About 5 to 10% of people lack stereo vision, often after a squint or lazy eye in childhood.
  • Timing Correcting the eyes after about age eight to ten does not restore stereo vision. Early treatment matters.
  • Living with it Other cues still give depth. Most stereo-blind people drive and play sports.

What the experiments show

  1. Single cells in V1 are tuned to near, far or zero disparity.
  2. Removing V4 or MT does not remove stereo vision, only slows it by tens of milliseconds.
  3. Removing MT does impair depth from motion parallax.
  4. Brain scans show depth cues activating many visual areas, some shared and some specific to one cue.
  5. Monkeys judge depth fastest and most accurately when stereo, parallax and shading are combined.

Depth is computed across many areas at once, and speed matters as much as accuracy.

Spreading the eyes apart

  • Hammerhead shark Eyes far apart magnify disparity.
  • Aerial photographs Cameras set wide apart revealed camouflaged objects rising from the ground.
  • Stereoscope A nineteenth-century viewer that shows each eye its own photograph, producing vivid depth.

Show one eye the first half of a word and the other eye the second half, and most people with stereo vision read the whole word. People who cannot fuse the two usually lack stereo vision.

Steps

  1. Hold a needle and thread, or a pen and its cap, in front of you.
  2. Put the cap on the pen with both eyes open, then with one eye closed.
  3. With one eye it is clumsy. Stereo vision is strongest within about three metres.
  4. Hold a thumb at arm's length and move your head from side to side.
  5. The thumb sweeps across the background faster than distant objects. This motion parallax works at any distance.
  6. Look at a photograph of a long road.
  7. Converging edges and shrinking objects give depth from perspective, even on a flat page.

Time. 5 minutes

Words to know

Binocular disparity
The small difference between where an object lands on the two retinas.
Motion parallax
Near objects sweeping across the retina faster than far ones when you or they move.
Amblyopia
Reduced vision in one eye from poor use in early childhood, often called lazy eye.
Strabismus
Eyes that do not point at the same place: turned in or out.
