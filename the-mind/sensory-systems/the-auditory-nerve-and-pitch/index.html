The Auditory Nerve and Pitch

What it is. About 30,000 nerve fibres carry sound from each ear. Each is tuned to a best frequency set by its place in the cochlea, and at low frequencies fibres also fire in step with the sound wave.

What it gives you. These two codes, place and timing, explain how finely we tell pitches apart, why octaves sound pure, and why a damaged ear hears but cannot understand.

Place and timing

Each nerve fibre connects to one inner hair cell at one place along the cochlea. At quiet levels it fires only near its best frequency. Labelling single fibres showed that frequency maps onto place in an orderly, logarithmic way.

Fibres also fire in time with the sound wave, at the same point in each cycle, though not on every cycle. This timing code is strong up to about 1 kHz and fades by about 3 to 5 kHz.

High pitches are coded by place alone; low pitches by place and timing. Most music sits below about 3 kHz, where timing is available, and octave matching becomes impossible above about 5 kHz.

Two kinds of nerve fibre

  • Type I Thick, myelinated, about 95% of fibres. Up to 20 contact each inner hair cell; each fibre contacts only one.
  • Type II Thin, unmyelinated, about 5%. Each branches to many outer hair cells. Their role is still unclear.
  • Ribbon synapse A dense ribbon at each hair cell synapse coordinates rapid, steady release, as in the eye.

What the nerve reveals

  • Tuning curves V-shaped plots of the quietest sound that drives a fibre, with a sharp tip at its best frequency.
  • Fine discrimination About 2 Hz at 1,000 Hz, even in untrained listeners.
  • Suppression A second tone just outside the tuning curve reduces the response. This happens in the mechanics, not by inhibition.
  • Masking curves Measured in people with a quiet probe tone, they match the nerve's tuning curves.
  • Octaves Notes an octave apart share many spike intervals, so they blend.
  • Stretched octaves Listeners set the upper note slightly sharp, because nerve recovery time shortens the shortest intervals.

Why louder is not clearer

  1. The sharp tip of each tuning curve disappears.
  2. Fibres need 40 to 60 dB more sound to respond.
  3. Each fibre now responds to a broad band of frequencies.
  4. Pitch discrimination worsens and background noise floods every channel.
  5. A hearing aid restores loudness but cannot restore the sharp tuning.

This is the common complaint: "I can hear you, but I cannot understand you."

The hearing nerve

  • 30,000 fibres per human ear
  • 1 million fibres per eye, for comparison
  • 2 Hz pitch difference heard at 1,000 Hz
  • 1 kHz up to which timing coding is strong
  • 3-5 kHz where timing coding fades out
  • 440 Hz the A that orchestras tune to

Orchestras tune to the oboe because its pitch is very stable. Everyone else matches that A at 440 Hz.

Safety. Keep any test sound at a comfortable level, and stop at once if your ears ring or hurt.

Steps

  1. Use a tone generator app at a comfortable, low volume.
  2. Play 1,000 Hz, then 1,005 Hz, then 1,002 Hz.
  3. Most people hear the difference down to a few hertz. That is roughly the step from one inner hair cell to its neighbour.
  4. Play 440 Hz together with 880 Hz, then 440 Hz with 890 Hz.
  5. The first pair blends into one pure sound; the second beats roughly. Octave partners share timing patterns in the nerve.

Time. 5 minutes

Words to know

Characteristic frequency
The frequency a nerve fibre responds to at the lowest sound level.
Phase locking
Spikes timed to the same point in each cycle of a sound wave.
Tonotopy
An orderly map of frequency, from low to high, kept through the hearing pathway.
Octave
A doubling of frequency, such as 440 Hz to 880 Hz.
