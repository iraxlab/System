Illusions and Visual Prostheses

What it is. Illusions reveal the rules the visual system uses, and those same rules shape how a prosthesis in the visual cortex has to be designed to restore some sight.

What it gives you. Illusions show that seeing is an active construction. A visual prosthesis is one of the main hopes for the more than 40 million blind people worldwide.

What illusions reveal

An illusion is not a fault. It shows a rule the visual system relies on, applied where it does not fit. Testing when an illusion appears and disappears tells us at which stage of the system it arises.

Two tests are especially useful. If an illusion still works when each part is shown to a different eye, it arises in the cortex, after the eyes combine. If it vanishes when the colours are made equally bright, it depends on brightness channels.

The same findings guide the design of visual prostheses. A good device has to place electrodes the way the cortex devotes space, with many more near the centre of gaze, and send signals mainly at edges, as cortical cells do.

What each one tells us

  • Hermann grid Grey spots at crossings. The textbook retinal explanation fails many tests; cortical cells are more likely.
  • Brightness contrast Equal greys look different on different backgrounds. Brightness is judged locally.
  • Cafe wall Offset tiles with grey mortar make parallel rows look tilted.
  • Arrow lengths Fins on a line change how long it looks. It survives one part per eye, so it is cortical.
  • Figure and ground A vase or two faces; an old woman or a young one. The brain flips between readings.
  • Waterfall After watching downward motion, still rocks seem to rise. Motion cells fatigue.

Illusions made in the retina

  • Afterimages Fixate a pattern and look away; a reversed image appears. Switching eyes removes it.
  • Colour ghosts A moving magenta spot leaves a green trail, the opposite colour.
  • Brightness masking A bright flash wipes out a dimmer one shown just before it, because bright signals travel faster.

Designing a visual prosthesis

  1. The retina and thalamus degenerate in many forms of blindness, so the visual cortex is a promising site.
  2. A small current in the visual cortex produces a phosphene, a faint star-like spot.
  3. Spacing electrodes evenly distorts shapes into a butterfly. Spacing them like the cortex keeps shapes true.
  4. A camera feeds software that activates electrodes only where edges fall, ignoring flat areas.
  5. In simulations, shapes and moving words become readable with a few hundred well-placed electrodes.

Long-term tissue reaction around electrodes remains a central problem.

Blindness and prostheses

  • 40 million+ blind people worldwide
  • 1 million+ blind people in the United States
  • 20-120 µA current that produces a phosphene in monkey cortex
  • 6-12% contrast of the spot it produces
  • 420 well-placed electrodes match 600+ evenly spaced ones
  • 3 needs for walking: pattern, motion and depth

Many illusions disappear when the two colours involved are set to equal brightness. That points to the brightness channels, not the colour channels, as their source.

Safety. Never look at the sun or a bright lamp, and stop if your eyes strain or water.

Steps

  1. Draw a grid of thick black squares with white lines between them, or find a Hermann grid online.
  2. Look at the grid without fixing on one crossing.
  3. Grey spots flicker at the crossings you are not looking at. The spot vanishes wherever you look directly.
  4. Look at two equal grey squares, one on a dark and one on a light background.
  5. The square on dark looks lighter. The visual system compares neighbours and judges brightness by contrast.

Time. 5 minutes

Words to know

Phosphene
A small spot of light seen when the visual cortex is stimulated electrically.
Motion aftereffect
After watching steady motion, still things appear to drift the other way.
Dichoptic
Showing a different image to each eye.
