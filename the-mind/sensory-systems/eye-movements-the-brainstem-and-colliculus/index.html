Eye Movements: The Brainstem and Colliculus

What it is. Six muscles move each eye. Brainstem neurons set eye position by their firing rate, and the superior colliculus holds a map that turns a place in the visual field into a saccade of the right size and direction.

What it gives you. We make about three quick eye movements every second, over 150,000 a day, each one a decision about where to look next.

Pointing a small, sharp centre

Only the fovea sees sharply, and it covers a tiny part of the scene. The eyes must jump constantly to bring targets onto it. Other movements track moving objects, keep the image steady while the head moves, and turn the eyes inward for near targets.

Each eye has six muscles, driven by three cranial nerves. The eye has no load to carry, which makes its control unusually clear. Motor neurons fire at a rate that matches eye position, add a burst to jump and fall silent to let the opposite muscle pull.

Above them, the superior colliculus works differently. A site in its map codes a movement of a fixed size and direction. Stimulate it longer and the eye makes repeated equal jumps instead of one larger jump.

Why the eyes move

  • Saccades Fast jumps that bring a target onto the fovea. About three per second.
  • Smooth pursuit Steady tracking of a moving target, such as a bird in flight.
  • Stabilisation Reflexes from the balance organs keep the image still while the head moves.
  • Vergence The eyes turn inward or outward together for near or far targets.
  • Optokinetic Following a moving scene, with a quick reset, as from a train window.
  • Fixation Holding the gaze steady on one point between movements.

Two codes for one movement

  • Rate code Brainstem motor neurons fire faster the further the eye turns. Burst length sets the size of a jump.
  • Vector code Each site in the colliculus codes one size and direction of movement, whatever the starting point.
  • Same neurons The same motor neurons drive jumps, pursuit and steady holding.

From seeing a target to moving towards it

  1. The top layer receives direct input from the retina and maps the opposite half of the visual field.
  2. The next layer receives input from the visual cortex.
  3. Deeper layers fire just before a jump of a particular size and direction.
  4. Each site's movement field lines up with its visual field, so seeing and moving share one map.
  5. In primates, the cortex drives the deeper layers. Without V1 they stop responding to light.

In fish and frogs the colliculus is the main visual centre. In primates it shrank as the cortex took over.

The eye in motion

  • 3 per second saccades while you look around
  • 150,000+ saccades a day
  • 6 muscles move each eye
  • 3 cranial nerves drive them
  • 90% accuracy of a single saccade; a small correction often follows
  • 5-6 mm size of the superior colliculus in primates

When people view a painting of a horse and rider, their first fixations go to the rider's eyes, then the horse's. Where we look reveals what we judge important.

Steps

  1. Ask someone to look at a picture of a face while you watch their eyes.
  2. Their eyes jump, not glide. Fixations cluster on the eyes, mouth and edges, rarely on smooth areas.
  3. Ask them to follow your slowly moving finger.
  4. Now the eyes glide smoothly. Pursuit needs a moving target; it cannot be done on an empty wall.
  5. Look at a word and shake your head gently.
  6. The word stays clear. A reflex moves the eyes against the head to keep the image steady.

Time. 5 minutes

Words to know

Saccade
A fast jump of the eyes from one point to the next.
Smooth pursuit
Slow, steady tracking of a moving object.
Superior colliculus
A layered structure on the roof of the midbrain that directs the eyes towards targets.
Vergence
The eyes turning inward or outward together to look near or far.
