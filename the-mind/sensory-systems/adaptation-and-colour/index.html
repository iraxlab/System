Adaptation and Colour

What it is. Three cone types with overlapping sensitivity code colour by their ratio, and the retina adapts to light levels that differ ten-billion-fold by signalling contrast instead of absolute light.

What it gives you. It explains colour blindness, afterimages, why white is a mixture, and how a page looks white both indoors and in sunshine.

Three cones and a ratio

Newton passed sunlight through a prism and found a band of colours. A second prism could not split any single colour further. White light, then, is a mixture of all visible wavelengths, not a pure colour.

The eye uses three cone types, most sensitive to short, medium and long wavelengths. Each responds broadly; colour is read from how strongly each type is driven relative to the others. Three receptors are enough to code thousands of hues.

Cells after the cones work in opposing pairs: red against green, blue against yellow, light against dark. This is why there is a yellowish green but never a reddish green, and why afterimages appear in the opposite colour.

Three dimensions

  • Hue Which colour it is: red, green, blue. Arranged around the colour circle.
  • Saturation How vivid it is, from grey at the centre of the circle to pure colour at the rim.
  • Brightness How light or dark it is, the third axis of the colour solid.

Colour and brightness are experiences. Wavelength and light intensity are their physical causes.

When a cone type is missing

  • Protan The long-wavelength (red) cones are missing or altered.
  • Deutan The medium-wavelength (green) cones are missing or altered.
  • Tritan The short-wavelength (blue) cones are missing. Rare.
  • Who About 8% of men of European descent, fewer in other groups, and about ten times fewer women.
  • Plate tests A number hidden in coloured dots is invisible to some people with colour deficiency.
  • Arrangement tests Coloured caps are put in order of hue; the pattern of errors shows the type.

Seeing at noon and at dusk

  1. Light levels across a day span about ten billion to one.
  2. The pupil adjusts by only about sixteen to one, so it contributes little.
  3. Most adjustment happens in the receptors, as their pigment is used up in bright light and restored in the dark.
  4. About 95% of ganglion cells report contrast, the difference from the background, rather than absolute light.
  5. So a white page sends a similar signal indoors and outdoors.

Hold an image perfectly still on the retina and it fades within a minute. Vision depends on change.

Where colour is processed

  • Midget system Silencing it removes colour vision entirely.
  • Cardinal axes Thalamus cells cluster on red-green and blue-yellow. In-between hues are computed in the cortex.
  • Many areas Damage to V4 leaves only small colour deficits. Colour is handled across several areas.

Blue cones are nearly absent from the very centre of the fovea. Fine blue text on black is harder to read than the same text in red or green.

Safety. Never look at the sun or a bright lamp, and stop if your eyes strain or water.

Steps

  1. Find a strongly coloured object, such as a red cup, on a plain white wall.
  2. Stare at its centre for 30 seconds without moving your eyes.
  3. Move your gaze to the white wall.
  4. A green ghost appears. Afterimages take the opposite colour across the colour circle.
  5. Repeat with one eye closed, then switch eyes when you look at the wall.
  6. The other eye sees no afterimage. Adaptation happens in the retina, not higher up.

Time. 3 minutes

Words to know

Trichromacy
Colour vision based on three cone types, with colour read from their relative activity.
Opponent colours
Red against green and blue against yellow: pairs that cancel and never mix into one hue.
Contrast
How much a target differs from its background, relative to the overall light level.
Isoluminance
Two colours of equal brightness, so they differ only in hue.
