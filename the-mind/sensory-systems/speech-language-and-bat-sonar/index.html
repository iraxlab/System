Speech, Language and Bat Sonar

What it is. Bats locate prey with echoes, and their cortex has cells tuned to the delay and pitch shift between call and echo. In humans, speech is decoded by a left-hemisphere network around the Sylvian fissure.

What it gives you. Comparing the two shows how a cortex specialises for the sounds that matter most to its owner, and explains common language disorders after stroke.

Seeing with sound

Echolocating bats send out calls far above human hearing and listen for echoes. The delay between call and echo gives distance. Near a target they call faster and faster, then fall silent while they eat.

Some tropical bats use a long constant tone. An echo from something moving returns slightly higher or lower in pitch, the Doppler shift, so they can pick out a fluttering moth among motionless leaves.

In their cortex, cells ignore a call or an echo alone and fire strongly to a call followed by an echo at one particular delay. Those delays are laid out in a map that corresponds to target distance.

Sweeps and steady tones

  • Sweeping bats Calls sweep from high to low pitch. They hunt in open air, over lakes and fields.
  • Steady-tone bats Long constant tones end in a short sweep. They hunt in dense forest and detect movement by Doppler shift.
  • Fine detection In early tests in darkness, bats avoided wires as thin as a human hair.

Specialised maps

  • Delay tuning Cells fire for call and echo separated by one delay, such as 9 ms.
  • Distance map Delays of 0.4 to 18 ms are mapped in order, matching targets 7 to 310 cm away.
  • Acoustic fovea A large part of the main map is given to 61 to 66 kHz, the pitch of the loudest call and its echoes.
  • Doppler area Another region compares the steady tone of the call with that of the echo.
  • Synthesised calls Researchers built artificial calls and echoes to find exactly which parts drive each cell.
  • Lower roots Some delay tuning already appears in the inferior colliculus.

How speech is built

  1. The vocal folds open and close, producing a buzz rich in harmonics.
  2. The tongue, jaw and lips shape the vocal tract into a filter with peaks called formants.
  3. Each vowel has its own formant pattern, held steady over time.
  4. Consonants add silences, noise bursts and fast formant sweeps.
  5. A small change in one sweep switches "da" to "ga" abruptly, with nothing heard in between.

Hearing continuous changes as sharp categories is called categorical perception.

A left-hemisphere network

  • Broca's area Damage gives slow, effortful speech with missing small words. Comprehension is better.
  • Wernicke's area Damage gives fluent speech but poor understanding.
  • A network Scans show both areas, and the cortex around them, active even when people only listen.

In most right-handers language sits in the left hemisphere. Left-handers vary.

How we know

  • Stroke Aphasia follows cortical damage; damage to the brainstem or thalamus rarely causes it.
  • One-sided anaesthesia Before epilepsy surgery, putting one hemisphere briefly to sleep shows which side holds language.
  • Scans A story in a known language lights up the left language network. An unknown language barely does.

Bats were long thought to avoid obstacles by touch. A student with an ultrasound detector first heard their calls in 1938, and named the ability echolocation.

Steps

  1. Say "da" and "ga" slowly, feeling your tongue.
  2. The difference lies in a fast frequency sweep at the start. Listeners hear one or the other, never a blend.
  3. Say "ahhh" and hold it, then say "father".
  4. Vowels can be held; stop consonants like "f" and "t" begin with a brief silence and a burst.
  5. Watch bats over a lake at dusk, if they live near you.
  6. They hunt in open air, where a single insect gives a single echo.

Time. 5 minutes

Words to know

Echolocation
Finding objects by sending out sounds and analysing their echoes.
Doppler shift
A rise in pitch from a source moving closer, and a fall as it moves away.
Formant
A peak in the speech spectrum created by the shape of the vocal tract.
Aphasia
A disorder of understanding or producing language after brain damage.
