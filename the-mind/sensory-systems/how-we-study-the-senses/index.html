How We Study the Senses

What it is. The senses are studied with a toolkit: careful behavioural tests, anatomy, recording single cells, stimulation, switching areas off, imaging and light-controlled genes.

What it gives you. Every claim about seeing and hearing in this part rests on one of these methods. Knowing them lets you judge how solid a finding is.

Two senses, one set of questions

Vision and hearing are the two senses we understand best. Both start with receptor cells that turn a physical signal into electrical activity, and both pass that activity through a chain of brain stations before it becomes experience.

The questions are the same for each sense. What does each station add? Which cells carry which kind of information? What is lost when a part is damaged? Answers come from combining several methods, because each one alone can mislead.

The eye maps space directly onto the retina. The ear maps frequency instead, so the brain has to compute where a sound is. That single difference shapes how the two systems are built, and it runs through the whole of this part.

How the senses are studied

  • Psychophysics Detection and discrimination tests show exactly what an observer can and cannot see or hear.
  • Anatomy Stains reveal cell bodies, whole neurons and the wiring between areas.
  • Recording Fine electrodes pick up single cells firing; played on a speaker, you can hear a receptive field.
  • Stimulation Current in the visual cortex makes a person see a point of light. In eye-movement areas it moves the eyes.
  • Inactivation Lesions, local anaesthetic or reversible cooling show what is lost when an area falls silent.
  • Imaging and optogenetics fMRI shows active areas. Light-sensitive proteins switch chosen cell types on or off.

Two basic tests

  • Detection One target appears. Its contrast, size or colour is lowered until it can no longer be found.
  • Discrimination One odd item sits among identical ones. The difference shrinks until the odd one cannot be picked out.
  • Fixation The observer holds the gaze on a spot, so each stimulus lands on a known place on the retina.

From air to nerve

  • Outer and middle ear The eardrum and three tiny bones carry vibration into the fluid-filled inner ear.
  • Cochlea A coiled tube where hair cells sit in order: high pitches at the base, low pitches at the tip.
  • Auditory nerve About 30,000 fibres per ear, each tuned to its own best frequency, carry the signal to the brainstem.

Eye and ear compared

  • 1 million+ nerve fibres leave each eye
  • 30,000 nerve fibres leave each ear
  • 3 per second quick eye movements while you look around
  • 20 Hz-20 kHz range of young human hearing
  • 1 ms timing that hearing nerve fibres can follow
  • 100 per second flashes before vision blurs them together

The cochlear implant is the most successful neural prosthesis in use. It bypasses lost hair cells and stimulates the hearing nerve directly, and many users follow speech without lip-reading.

Safety. Never look at the sun or a bright lamp, and stop if your eyes strain or water.

Steps

  1. Pick a small dot on a wall about two metres away and keep your eyes on it.
  2. Hold a coloured pen beside your head and bring it slowly forward from behind.
  3. You notice movement before you can name the colour. The edge of the retina detects change but resolves little detail.
  4. Now read a line of text and pay attention to your eyes.
  5. They move in quick jumps, about three per second. The sharp centre of the eye has to be pointed at each word.

Time. 5 minutes

Words to know

Psychophysics
Measuring what a person or animal can detect or tell apart, under tightly controlled conditions.
Receptive field
The patch of the visual field, or band of sound frequencies, that changes a given cell's firing.
Optogenetics
Placing light-sensitive proteins into chosen cells so that light can switch those cells on or off.
