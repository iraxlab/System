Motion Perception and Pursuit

What it is. Cells in V1 and especially in area MT respond to particular directions and speeds of motion. Motion reveals hidden shapes and drives the eyes to track moving objects.

What it gives you. Motion breaks camouflage, tells us how others move, and keeps the image steady while we travel. Films and screens work only because the brain sees apparent motion.

Cells that care about direction

Most cells in the primary visual cortex respond to motion in one direction more than the opposite. In area MT nearly every cell does, and motion the wrong way actively inhibits it. MST, beyond MT, has fields tens of degrees wide that remain tightly tuned.

Direction tuning is built by lopsided inhibition. In the rabbit retina, blocking an inhibitory transmitter removes it, and it returns when the drug washes out. The same principle is thought to work in the cortex.

Without MT, monkeys struggle to see moving dots and flicker, even colour flicker. Without V4 they do not. Motion and flicker depend on the parasol route that runs through MT.

Straight, circular, expanding

  • Straight An object crossing the field.
  • Circular A spinning wheel.
  • Expanding The scene streaming outward as you drive forward; contracting as you reverse.

Most MST cells respond to two or three of these. Perception comes from groups of cells, not single detectors.

What movement adds

  • Breaking camouflage A still insect on bark is invisible until it moves.
  • Points of light A dozen lights on a person's joints look random when still, and like a walking person when moving.
  • Apparent motion Two dots flashing in turn look like one dot jumping.
  • Global rules Many separate ambiguous displays all flip direction together, as if one decision rules them.
  • Backward wheels Film shows fast wheels turning the wrong way. This never happens in continuous daylight.
  • Flicker Early films at 16 frames per second flickered. Showing each frame three times removed it.

Following a moving scene

  1. A moving scene drives slow tracking of the eyes.
  2. A quick jump resets them, and the cycle repeats.
  3. Special retinal cells tuned to slow motion feed a dedicated pathway.
  4. Their three preferred directions match the three canals of the balance organ.
  5. The signal passes through the cerebellum to the eye-muscle nuclei.

If one eye is held still, the other eye tracks faster and faster, because the feedback loop is broken.

When one image hides another

  • Ring after disc A ring shown about 60-100 ms after a disc hides it, even when each goes to a different eye. This arises in the cortex.
  • Bright after dim A bright disc wipes out a dimmer one shown just before. It fails across the eyes, so it arises in the retina.
  • Why Brighter signals travel faster and overtake the dimmer signal on the way to the brain.

From a few moving lights on the joints of two dancers in the dark, viewers can tell which dancer is the man and which the woman.

Safety. Never look at the sun or a bright lamp, and stop if your eyes strain or water.

Steps

  1. Watch a video of a car or carriage wheel accelerating.
  2. At some speed the wheel seems to turn backwards. Each frame shows the nearest spoke slightly behind its earlier place.
  3. Stare at a waterfall or scrolling text for 30 seconds, then look at a still wall.
  4. The wall seems to drift the other way. Cells tuned to the first direction have tired.
  5. Look out of a moving train window.
  6. Your eyes follow the scene, then flick back, again and again. This is the optokinetic reflex.

Time. 5 minutes

Words to know

Direction selectivity
A cell firing for motion in one direction and not the reverse.
Apparent motion
Seeing smooth movement from a sequence of still images, as in film.
Optokinetic nystagmus
Slow tracking of a moving scene followed by a quick reset, repeated.
MST
An area beyond MT with very large fields, tuned to straight, circular and expanding motion.
