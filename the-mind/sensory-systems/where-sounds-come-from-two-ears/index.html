Where Sounds Come From: Two Ears

What it is. The brain locates sounds left and right by comparing the two ears: tiny time differences at low frequencies and level differences at high frequencies. Height and front-back come from the shape of the outer ear.

What it gives you. We can detect a shift of about one degree, which needs timing differences of about ten millionths of a second. This shows how finely the brainstem compares two inputs.

Two ears, two cues

A sound off to one side reaches the nearer ear first. For a human head the largest delay, with the sound straight to the side, is about 0.6 thousandths of a second. The head also casts a sound shadow, so the far ear hears it more quietly.

Low frequencies bend round the head, so they give almost no level difference; the brain uses timing. High frequencies give big level differences, up to about 20 dB, but their waves are so short that ongoing timing becomes ambiguous above about 1.5 kHz.

Neither cue changes with height, and both are the same in front and behind. The folds of the outer ear fill these gaps by shaping the sound spectrum. Height judgements are much coarser than left and right.

Timing, level and ear shape

  • Time difference Used at low frequencies. The smallest detectable change is about 10 microseconds.
  • Level difference Used at high frequencies. The smallest detectable change is about 1 dB.
  • Ear shape Gives height and front versus back. Works with one ear, but only to about 10 to 30 degrees.

How good we are

  • 1 degree smallest detectable shift straight ahead, at low frequencies
  • 3 degrees at middle frequencies, where both cues are weak
  • 0.6 ms largest time difference for a human head
  • 10 µs smallest detectable time difference
  • 20 dB largest level difference at high frequencies
  • 340 m/s speed of sound in air

A model from 1948

  1. Cells in the medial superior olive receive input from both ears on two separate dendrites.
  2. They fire strongly only when both inputs arrive together.
  3. Input fibres act as delay lines, reaching each cell along a row a little later.
  4. Each cell therefore matches one time difference, one direction in space.
  5. The row of cells forms a map of sound direction.

In birds this design is clear. In mammals, inhibition and other delay sources also play a role, and the debate continues.

The limits of the code

  • Phase locking Nerve fibres follow the wave only up to about 1 kHz. Above that, timing becomes unreliable.
  • Headphones Sounds in headphones seem inside the head, because the outer ear and the room are missing.
  • Implants Two cochlear implants do not keep fine timing and are not synchronised, so time cues are mostly lost.

A spike lasts about one thousandth of a second, yet the brain compares spikes from the two ears to within ten millionths of a second, a hundred times finer.

Safety. Keep any test sound at a comfortable level, and stop at once if your ears ring or hurt.

Steps

  1. Close your eyes and ask a friend to jingle keys at different places around you.
  2. Point to each sound, then open your eyes to check.
  3. Most people land within about 20 degrees.
  4. Repeat with one ear blocked.
  5. Errors grow. Comparing two ears is the main cue for left and right.
  6. Repeat with one ear blocked and the other ear folded forward.
  7. The sound seems to come from everywhere. Ear shape is needed for height and for front versus back.

Time. 5 minutes

Words to know

ITD
Interaural time difference: how much earlier a sound reaches one ear than the other.
ILD
Interaural level difference: how much louder a sound is in the nearer ear.
Azimuth
Direction in the horizontal plane, from left through front to right.
Coincidence detector
A cell that fires only when inputs from both sides arrive at the same moment.
