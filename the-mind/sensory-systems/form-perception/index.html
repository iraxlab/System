Form Perception

What it is. Recognising shapes depends on whole patterns, not on adding up pieces. The brain uses coarse and fine detail, grouping rules and broadly tuned cells spread over many areas.

What it gives you. It explains why a face is still recognisable in a sketch of dots, why upside-down faces are hard, and why pixelating a face hides who it is.

More than the sum of its parts

Around 1900, one school held that perception is a sum of basic sensations, like bricks in a wall. The list of elements grew past 40,000 and the idea collapsed. The Gestalt school replied that we see wholes first: a car behind railings is still a car.

Three brain theories followed. Shapes are built from line segments; the cortex holds a picture that the mind reads; or the visual system analyses scenes into stripes of different fineness. None explains everything, but the third has the most support.

In higher areas, single cells respond best to some shapes but also to many others. A shape is coded by the pattern across tens of thousands of broadly tuned cells, not by one cell per object.

How parts become wholes

  • Proximity Items close together are seen as a group.
  • Similarity Items alike in colour or shape group together.
  • Common motion Items moving together belong together.
  • Closure Gaps are filled so outlines look complete.
  • Figure and ground One region is seen as object, the other as background, and they can flip.
  • Continuity Smooth lines are followed through crossings.

Why a picture in the cortex cannot be the answer

  • Distortion The centre of gaze takes far more cortex, so the cortical copy of a shape is badly distorted.
  • Split A circle on the midline lands in two separate halves of the brain, yet looks round.
  • Equal arrows Two equal arrows produce very unequal activity, yet look equal.

Detail at different scales

  • Dot portraits Faces drawn only with dots are easily recognised, even blurred. Coarse detail carries identity.
  • Pixelation Blocky pixels add sharp false edges that mask the coarse pattern, so the face is hidden.
  • Separate channels Staring at stripes of one fineness lowers sensitivity to that fineness only.

Areas that help with shape

  1. V1 cells respond to orientation and fineness of pattern.
  2. Some V2 cells respond to illusory edges, where no line is drawn.
  3. Without V4, monkeys struggle to match shapes that are resized, partly hidden or faint.
  4. V4 also helps select a subtle target instead of the most eye-catching one.
  5. Inferotemporal cortex holds cells tuned to complex shapes and faces.

How the brain settles an ambiguous figure, such as a vase that is also two faces, is still unknown.

Around 1430, European paintings jumped to near-photographic realism. One argument holds that artists used lenses to project scenes onto the canvas; a clue is the number of left-handed sitters, as projected images are reversed.

Steps

  1. Find a photograph of a well-known face.
  2. Look at it with your eyes half closed, so it blurs.
  3. You still know who it is. Coarse detail carries much of identity.
  4. Turn the photograph upside down.
  5. Recognition becomes slow and uncertain. The face system is tuned to upright faces.
  6. Write the same word in three different handwritings and sizes.
  7. You read each instantly. Recognition holds across size, style and position.

Time. 5 minutes

Words to know

Gestalt
The view that a whole is perceived differently from the sum of its parts.
Illusory contour
An edge you see where no edge is drawn, completed by the brain.
Constancy
Seeing an object as the same despite changes in size, angle or light.
