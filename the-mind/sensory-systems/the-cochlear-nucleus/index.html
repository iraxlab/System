The Cochlear Nucleus

What it is. The cochlear nucleus is the first brain station for sound. Each incoming fibre branches to several regions, where about ten cell types split the signal into parallel streams.

What it gives you. Unlike vision, hearing passes through several brainstem stations, mainly to work out where sounds come from. The streams start here.

Where the streams begin

The auditory nerve ends in the cochlear nucleus, on the outside of the brainstem. Each fibre splits into branches that reach its ventral and dorsal parts, arranged by frequency, so the map from the cochlea is copied several times.

From here the pathway climbs through the superior olive, the inferior colliculus and the thalamus to the auditory cortex. Vision has no such chain of brainstem stations. Hearing needs them because the ear maps frequency, not space, and direction must be computed.

About ten cell types live in the nucleus, each with its own shape and firing pattern. Their outputs leave by separate tracts, forming parallel pathways much like the channels of the visual system.

From cochlea to cortex

  • Cochlea → Spiral ganglion → Cochlear nucleus
  • Cochlear nucleus → Superior olive → Inferior colliculus
  • Cochlear nucleus → Inferior colliculus
  • Inferior colliculus → Medial geniculate → Auditory cortex
  • Higher centres → Cochlear nucleus: descending control
  • Superior olive → Cochlea: descending control

Shape and firing pattern

  • Bushy cells Fire much like the nerve itself. Spherical bushy cells feed the timing comparison between the ears.
  • Stellate cells Fire in a regular, rhythmic pattern while the sound lasts.
  • Octopus cells Dendrites spread to one side like tentacles. They fire a single spike at the onset.
  • Fusiform cells In the layered dorsal part. They fire, pause about 10 ms, then fire again.
  • Giant synapse The endbulb of Held wraps a bushy cell, passing timing with very little delay.
  • About ten types Most have no known function yet. Each likely feeds its own pathway.

How cell types were matched to signals

  • Two names Cells were named once by cell-body stains and again by whole-cell stains. Modern names combine both.
  • Firing patterns Hundreds of short tones and early computers gave four main patterns: nerve-like, rhythmic, pausing and onset.
  • Single cells filled Recording one cell and filling it with dye linked each pattern to a shape.

Stimulating the nucleus directly

  • Who People without a working auditory nerve, often after removal of nerve tumours.
  • How A flat pad of 15 to 21 electrodes sits on the surface of the nucleus.
  • Results Speech is rarely understood by sound alone; the device mainly helps lip-reading.

A Spanish anatomist's detailed drawings of these cells, made in the 1920s and 30s, waited about 30 years before they were published, and are still used today.

Safety. Keep any test sound at a comfortable level, and stop at once if your ears ring or hurt.

Steps

  1. Clap once in a small room and once in a large hall or stairwell.
  2. You hear the sharp onset clearly in both. Some cells fire one spike at the very start of a sound.
  3. Listen to a steady hum, such as a fridge.
  4. Other cells fire steadily for as long as a sound lasts, some in a regular rhythm.
  5. Cover one ear and listen to a sound off to the side, then uncover it.
  6. Direction becomes clearer with both ears. Bushy cells send exact timing to the next station, where the ears are compared.

Time. 3 minutes

Words to know

Nucleus
A cluster of nerve cells inside the brain. In the periphery the same thing is called a ganglion.
Endbulb of Held
A giant nerve ending that wraps around a bushy cell; the largest synapse in the brain.
PST histogram
A plot of spike timing averaged over hundreds of repeats of the same sound.
