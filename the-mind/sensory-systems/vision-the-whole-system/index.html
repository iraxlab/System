Vision: The Whole System

What it is. A summary of the visual system from eye to eye movements: parallel channels from the retina, feature analysis in the cortex, many specialised but overlapping areas, and two routes that point the eyes.

What it gives you. Seeing the whole system at once shows its logic: split the signal early, analyse it in parallel, and combine the results to guide action.

The visual system in one view

The eye splits its output early. ON and OFF cells report light and dark changes; midget and parasol cells report detail and colour versus motion and flicker. Each eye's signals go to separate layers of the thalamus and meet in the visual cortex.

The cortex analyses features: orientation, direction, fineness, colour and depth. More than 30 areas with over 300 connections share the work. No area has a single job, but damage reveals strong leanings: MT for motion and flicker, V4 for choosing subtle targets.

Throughout, the system codes contrast rather than absolute light, because cells can fire at most a few hundred times a second while light varies ten-billion-fold. And it moves the eyes about three times a second to keep the sharp fovea on what matters.

From light to action

  • Retina → Thalamus → Primary visual cortex
  • Primary visual cortex → V2 → V4 and temporal lobe: form and objects
  • Primary visual cortex → MT → Parietal lobe: motion and space
  • Retina → Superior colliculus: orienting
  • Cortex → Superior colliculus → Brainstem: eye movements
  • Frontal eye fields → Brainstem: eye movements

Six principles of vision

  • Contrast, not light Most cells report differences from the background.
  • Parallel channels ON and OFF, midget and parasol, each with its own range.
  • Feature analysis V1 breaks the image into edges, directions and stripe widths.
  • Distributed areas Colour, form and depth are handled across many areas.
  • Maps Areas keep a map of the visual field, with the centre greatly enlarged.
  • Action Vision exists to guide behaviour, starting with where to look.

What each loss tells us

  • Midget system Colour, fine detail, texture and fine depth are lost.
  • MT Motion and flicker suffer badly; depth from stereo survives.
  • V4 Basic tasks recover, but picking subtle targets and learning new shapes stay hard.

Vision at a glance

  • 10 billion : 1 range of light levels we see in
  • 400-600 maximum spikes per second of a ganglion cell
  • 30+ visual areas in the primate cortex
  • 300+ connections between them
  • 10% of people lack stereo vision
  • 18 degrees beyond which the thalamus relay has four layers instead of six

A single rod holds about a thousand stacked discs, each with some ten thousand pigment molecules. The discs are renewed continually throughout life.

Steps

  1. Sit in a room with a window and a patterned object in view.
  2. Look from the window to the object and back.
  3. Your pupils, receptors and contrast coding adjust within seconds. A white page stays white in both light levels.
  4. Look at the patterned object and name its colour, shape and distance.
  5. Parallel channels carried each quality, and the cortex combined them in under a fifth of a second.
  6. Count how often your eyes jump while you look around the room for ten seconds.
  7. About 30 jumps, each one chosen by the eye-movement circuits.

Time. 5 minutes

Words to know

Parallel processing
Several channels handling different aspects of the same input at the same time.
Encephalisation
The shift of functions from older brainstem centres to the cortex over evolution.
Cortical magnification
The much larger area of cortex given to the centre of gaze than to the periphery.
