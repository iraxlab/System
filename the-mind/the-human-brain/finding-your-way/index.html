Finding Your Way

What it is. Navigation requires answering two questions continuously: where am I, and how do I reach my goal from here?

What it gives you. This identifies the brain systems that keep us oriented, and explains why some people are excellent navigators while others get lost easily.

Where am I, and how do I get there?

Many animals navigate across extraordinary distances with small brains. Humans rely on a combination of landmarks, the geometry of the space around them and an internal sense of direction.

A region on the underside of the brain, the parahippocampal place area, responds strongly to scenes. It reacts to the shape of the space: walls, corners and open areas. An empty room activates it as much as a furnished one.

Other regions track which way we face and how far we have moved. Together they build a cognitive map, which lets us find shortcuts we have never taken.

Small brains, remarkable journeys

  1. A monarch butterfly, weighing half a gram, flies more than 3,000 km to a few hectares of forest in Mexico.
  2. The round trip spans four generations. Descendants return to the same trees their ancestors left.
  3. Loggerhead turtles return, after up to 20 years, to the beach where they hatched.
  4. Bats maintain their sense of direction while flying upside down in complete darkness.
  5. A desert ant forages far from its nest, then runs straight home. Displaced, it runs the same vector from the new spot.

The ant tracks its own steps and turns, using no landmarks at all.

Six things a navigator needs

  • Recognition Identifying a familiar place, or at least what kind of place it is.
  • Spatial layout The geometry of the surroundings: walls, exits and their distances.
  • Heading Which direction one faces. Leaving a subway station, you may know the corner but not the compass direction.
  • A cognitive map Rats trained on a winding route take a new direct path as soon as it opens.
  • Reorientation A way to reset after becoming lost.
  • Navigable paths Where movement is actually possible. Glass can be seen through but not walked through.

What drives its response

  • Furnished room Strong response.
  • Empty room Equally strong.
  • Furniture without walls About half the response.
  • Map seen from above Weak response.
  • Scrambled room photo Weak response.
  • Room built from LEGO Strong response.

It represents the shape of space, not the objects within it.

When surgeons applied a small current to one patient's place area, he reported seeing a train station, a staircase and a closet full of blue objects.

Safety. Stand still with your eyes closed; never walk with your eyes shut.

Steps

  1. Stand still in a room you know well.
  2. Close your eyes and point to the door, the window and the far wall.
  3. Estimate the distance to each.
  4. Open your eyes and check. You carry a spatial model of the room with you.
  5. Walk an unfamiliar route once, then sketch it from memory.
  6. Note which turns and landmarks you retained. These are the anchors of your mental map.

Time. 10 minutes

Words to know

Cognitive map
An internal representation of the layout of an environment, allowing new routes to be planned.
Path integration
Tracking position by adding up one's own movements and turns, without landmarks.
Parahippocampal place area
A region that responds to the spatial layout of scenes, not to the objects in them.
