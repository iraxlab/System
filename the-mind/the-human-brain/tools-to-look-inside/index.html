Tools to Look Inside

What it is. Studying any mental ability starts with defining the problem the brain is solving, then testing how it is solved with behavioural experiments and brain imaging.

What it gives you. This approach explains why colour, face recognition and brain scans each reveal a different side of the same system, and why careful questions matter more than expensive machines.

Start with the problem

A useful way to study any ability is to ask three questions in order. What problem is the brain solving, and why is it hard? What steps does the mind follow to solve it? And how do neurons carry out those steps?

Colour shows why the first question matters. The light reaching the eye mixes the colour of a surface with the colour of the light falling on it. Two unknowns, one measurement. The brain resolves this by estimating the lighting first, then inferring the surface.

Faces show the value of behavioural tests. Most people recognise familiar faces instantly from any photo, yet struggle to match two different photos of a stranger. Brain imaging then shows where in the brain this expertise lives.

Three levels of explanation

  • The problem What goes in, what must come out, and what makes the task difficult. This comes before any experiment.
  • The method The sequence of steps the mind follows. Well-designed behavioural tests reveal them.
  • The mechanism How neurons and circuits physically carry out those steps.

Studying feathers alone never explains flight. You also need aerodynamics.

Four cars, one grey

  1. Four photos show the same car under coloured light: teal, purple, blue and yellow.
  2. Viewers confidently name four different car colours: red, green, yellow and blue.
  3. Mask everything except the car body, and all four patches are the same grey.
  4. The light reaching the eye combines surface colour and illumination colour.
  5. The brain estimates the illumination from the scene, then subtracts it to recover the surface.

Colour is the brain's inference about surfaces, and it is usually correct.

Faces and imaging

  • 2% of people have lifelong difficulty recognising faces, even of family
  • 40% of an infant's waking time includes a face in view
  • 7.5 vs 2 average groups formed when sorting photos of two strangers versus two familiar people
  • 6 sec delay before the fMRI blood signal peaks
  • 1 mm finest spatial detail in standard brain scans
  • 10 min to locate the face area in most people with fMRI

Primates with three types of colour receptor find ripe fruit against foliage faster than those with two. Colour vision likely evolved for exactly this task.

Steps

  1. Take a sheet of white paper.
  2. Look at it under a warm yellow lamp, then beside a window in daylight.
  3. It looks white both times, although the light reaching your eye differs greatly. The brain discounts the colour of the light.
  4. Find two photos of one stranger and two photos of a well-known person.
  5. The familiar pair matches instantly; the stranger is surprisingly hard. Face expertise applies mainly to faces we know.

Time. 10 minutes

Words to know

Ill-posed problem
A problem with more unknowns than available information. The brain solves it by building in assumptions about the world.
Prosopagnosia
Face blindness: seeing a face clearly but being unable to recognise whose it is.
BOLD signal
The blood-oxygen change that fMRI measures. It rises a few seconds after neurons become active.
