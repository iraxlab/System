Music

What it is. Music appears in every known human culture and is processed by dedicated neural populations, separate from those for language.

What it gives you. This places music among core human capacities rather than as a cultural luxury, and raises the question of why it evolved.

A universal human capacity

Music is ancient and universal. Bone flutes date back at least 40,000 years, and surveys of hundreds of cultures find that nearly all use a small set of pitches and a regular beat.

In the brain, music and language are largely separate. Regions that respond to sentences show little response to melody, and music-selective regions show little response to speech. The music response is present even in people with no musical training.

Why music evolved is still debated. It may have served courtship, infant care or social bonding, or it may be a by-product of abilities that evolved for other purposes.

Old, universal and early

  • 40,000 years: age of the oldest known bone flutes
  • 304 cultures surveyed: nearly all use set pitches and a beat
  • 4% of people are born with amusia
  • 200 ms for a newborn's brain to register a missing beat
  • 8 months later, an infant still recognises a learned melody
  • 12 months age at which unfamiliar rhythms stop feeling natural

Three explanations for music

  • Courtship Darwin proposed that early humans sang to attract mates before language developed.
  • Infant care Song reassures an infant that a caregiver is near, even when their hands are busy.
  • By-product Music may exploit abilities evolved for other purposes, a view summed up as "auditory cheesecake".

A dedicated system

  1. Language regions show little response to music; music regions show little response to sentences.
  2. Scanning people listening to 165 everyday sounds revealed separate speech and music components.
  3. The music component is present even in people with no musical training.
  4. Some neural populations respond only to song.
  5. In a remote Bolivian community, people show no preference for consonant over dissonant chords. That preference is learned.

People with congenital amusia also struggle to hear whether a spoken sentence is a question.

At five months, infants recognise a melody they have learned, even when it is sung higher or lower than before.

Steps

  1. Choose a well-known song.
  2. Hum it starting on a low note, then on a high note.
  3. It remains the same song. You recognise the intervals between notes, not the absolute pitches. This relative pitch is rare in other species.
  4. Clap a steady beat with another person, then have one of you skip a clap.
  5. Both notice the gap immediately. Even sleeping newborns show a brain response to a missing beat.

Time. 5 minutes

Words to know

Relative pitch
Recognising a melody by the intervals between its notes, regardless of starting pitch.
Amusia
A deficit in perceiving melody, present from birth or acquired after brain damage.
Consonance
The perception of note combinations as smooth or pleasant. Preference for it is largely learned.
