Numbers

What it is. Humans sense approximate quantity at a glance, without counting. Infants and many animals share this ability.

What it gives you. This number sense is the foundation for later mathematics, and its strength in early childhood predicts arithmetic skill years later.

Quantity without counting

Glance at two groups of dots and you can tell which is larger without counting. The judgement is fast and approximate, and it follows a simple rule: the larger the ratio between the groups, the easier the choice.

This ability appears in newborns and across many species, from fish to bees. It works across the senses: matching a number of sounds to a number of dots is nearly as easy as comparing dots with dots.

Exact counting and arithmetic are cultural inventions built on top of this ancient system. Neurons in parietal cortex respond to specific quantities, providing a biological basis for number.

Ratio, not difference

  • 16 vs 32 easy to distinguish at a glance
  • 16 vs 17 almost impossible without counting
  • 4 days old: infants match 12 sounds to 12 shapes
  • 1 million neurons in a bee brain that can add and subtract one
  • 30 the age at which number sense is sharpest
  • 4 years how far ahead early estimation skill predicts arithmetic

Quantity in the wild

  1. Fish join the larger of two schools, which lowers their individual risk.
  2. Lions assess how many rivals are calling before deciding whether to approach.
  3. Male túngara frogs answer a rival's call by adding one extra note.
  4. Rats trained on two or four flashes, given two flashes plus two tones, choose the "four" lever.
  5. Bees learn that blue means add one and yellow means subtract one.

Bees even treat an empty card as less than one.

How number sense works

  • Ratio-based The same rule governs judgements of brightness, weight and loudness.
  • Cross-modal Comparing dots with tones is nearly as accurate as comparing dots with dots.
  • A mental number line Asked whether 63 is larger than 65, people slow down as numbers get closer.

In parietal cortex, individual neurons are tuned to preferred quantities such as two or six.

A child's speed at estimating dots in kindergarten predicts arithmetic ability four years later, but not reading or general problem-solving.

Steps

  1. Take two handfuls of dried beans or coins.
  2. Spill them into two piles. Without counting, judge which is larger.
  3. Repeat with piles that are closer in size.
  4. Easy when one is double the other, hard when they are close. Number sense depends on ratio, not absolute difference.
  5. Count both piles to check your estimate.

Time. 5 minutes

Words to know

Approximate number system
The innate ability to estimate quantity without counting.
Weber's law
The ease of telling two quantities apart depends on their ratio, not their difference.
Dyscalculia
A specific, lifelong difficulty with numbers, unrelated to general intelligence.
