Meet Your Brain

What it is. The brain is not one uniform organ. It is a set of specialised regions, each with a main task, that together produce perception, thought and behaviour.

What it gives you. Knowing that the mind is built from parts explains why a person can be excellent at one skill and impaired at another, and why a small injury can remove one ability while leaving the rest intact.

A system of specialists

For centuries it was debated whether the brain works as a single whole or as a collection of parts. The evidence now strongly favours parts. Specific regions handle faces, places, language, music and the thoughts of other people.

This does not mean each region works alone. Regions are linked by long fibre tracts and constantly exchange information. But damage to one region often removes one function cleanly, which is the clearest proof that the functions are separate.

This part follows that idea through the whole brain: how it is built, the methods used to study it, and the specialised systems for seeing, navigating, counting, hearing, music, language and understanding others.

Tap a part to meet it

  • Planning (Front) Prefrontal cortex. Plans, weighs choices, holds goals in mind and inhibits impulsive responses.
  • Speaking (Front, left side) Broca's area, usually on the left. Organises words into sentences and coordinates the movements of speech.
  • Moving and touch (Top middle) Two parallel strips. The motor strip sends commands to the muscles; the strip behind it receives touch from the body.
  • Numbers and attention (Top back) Parietal cortex. Estimates quantity at a glance and directs attention to where it is needed.
  • Seeing (Back) The occipital lobe. Processes everything seen, from edges and colour to motion and form.
  • Hearing and music (Side, above the ear) Auditory cortex. Turns sound into recognisable voices, words and melodies.
  • Word meaning (Side, left) Language cortex in the left temporal lobe. Extracts the meaning of words and sentences.
  • Other minds (Side, back) A region near the junction of the temporal and parietal lobes that represents what other people think and believe.
  • Faces and places (Underneath) On the underside of the brain. One patch responds to faces, a neighbouring one to places and scenes.
  • Memory and inner map (Deep inside) The hippocampus. Forms new memories and maintains an internal map of the environment.
  • Balance (Back, underneath) The cerebellum. Keeps movement smooth, timed and balanced.
  • Breathing and heartbeat (Brainstem, the stalk) The brainstem. Runs breathing, heart rate and arousal continuously, without conscious control.

The left side of the brain. Dashed parts sit underneath or deep inside.

The man who kept getting lost

  1. Bob is articulate, sociable and successful, yet he repeatedly gets lost, even on familiar routes in his own town.
  2. After a fall, a scan finds a tumour about the size of a lime near the centre of the brain.
  3. It sits beside a region that responds specifically to places and scenes.
  4. He draws a bicycle accurately but cannot sketch the layout of his own home.
  5. Surgery succeeds and he returns to work within days. His sense of direction never recovers; he relies on a phone map.

One region failed. Every other ability stayed intact. This is the logic of functional specialisation.

Six ideas that run through this part

  • The brain has parts It is not a uniform mass. Distinct regions carry out distinct functions.
  • Some parts are highly specific Separate regions for faces and for places. Damage to one leaves the other working.
  • Brain structure reflects the mind Each specialised region reveals one component of how human thought is organised.
  • The brain changes It develops with age and adapts with experience. Young brains recover from injury best.
  • The brain is the self Hearts can be transplanted. A brain transplant would, in effect, transplant the person.
  • Multiple methods Behavioural tests, scans and patient studies are combined, because each shows only part of the picture.

Six ways to study the living brain

  • Behavioural tests Present a stimulus, record the response and its timing. Simple, cheap and often revealing.
  • Structural MRI A detailed image of the brain's anatomy, without radiation.
  • Functional MRI Shows which regions increase activity during a task, a few seconds after it happens.
  • Patient studies What is lost, and what remains, after damage to a specific region.
  • EEG and MEG Record electrical and magnetic signals from outside the head, with millisecond timing.
  • Tractography Maps the long fibre bundles that connect regions to each other.

Computer vision systems can now label the objects in a photograph with high accuracy, yet still struggle to understand why a scene is funny. The human brain does both without effort.

Safety. Stop if your eyes feel strained.

Steps

  1. Take a sheet of paper and a pen.
  2. Draw the floor plan of your home from memory, every room in place.
  3. Then draw a bicycle with its wheels, frame, seat, pedals and handlebars.
  4. Both drawings rely on different systems: one for spatial layout, one for objects. Either can fail alone.
  5. Watch moving water or a scrolling video for 30 seconds, then look at a still wall.
  6. The wall appears to drift the opposite way. This is the motion aftereffect: motion-selective neurons adapt and briefly under-respond.

Time. 10 minutes

Words to know

Cortex
The thin, folded outer layer of the brain, about 2 to 4 mm thick, where most perception and thought take place.
Functional specialisation
The principle that distinct brain regions are dedicated to distinct tasks.
MRI and fMRI
MRI images the structure of the brain; functional MRI tracks changes in blood oxygen to show which regions are active during a task.
