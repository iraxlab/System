More Tools

What it is. Each research method answers a different question: when the brain responds, where it responds, and whether a region is actually necessary for a task.

What it gives you. Knowing what each method can and cannot show makes it possible to judge whether a brain finding is strong or overstated.

Where, when and whether

No single method answers every question. fMRI shows where activity occurs, but the blood signal lags several seconds behind. EEG and MEG capture timing to the millisecond, but locating the source is difficult.

Recordings made directly from the brain during epilepsy surgery offer both exact location and timing. They are rare and depend on patients who agree to take part while electrodes are already in place for medical reasons.

Only damage or stimulation can show that a region is necessary. A scan shows correlation: a region becomes active. Disrupting that region and observing what fails shows causation.

Six methods and their limits

  • Behavioural tests Inexpensive and often ingenious, but they show only the output, not the process.
  • fMRI Good spatial detail, but slow: the signal trails neural activity by seconds.
  • EEG Millisecond timing, but the signal is blurred by skull and scalp.
  • MEG Similar timing to EEG, with somewhat better localisation.
  • Intracranial electrodes Exact in place and time. Available only during clinical procedures.
  • Lesions and stimulation The only methods that test whether a region is truly required.

EEG is like a microphone on a stadium roof: it picks up the crowd's roar, not individual conversations.

Measured in milliseconds

  • 150 ms to tell whether a photo contains an animal
  • 170 ms when a face-specific EEG wave appears
  • 130 ms when face-selective neurons begin to fire
  • 500,000 neurons inside one fMRI voxel
  • 2 mm size of a typical surgical recording electrode
  • 4 single face-selective neurons found in one human study

Proof from opposite cases

  1. A stroke damages one man's face area. He cannot recognise faces, yet identifies objects and voices normally.
  2. Another patient loses object recognition. He cannot tell a chair from a toaster but knows every face.
  3. Together these opposite cases show that faces and objects rely on separate systems.
  4. During surgery in Japan, a small current applied to the face area made the doctor's face appear to distort.
  5. When the patient looked at a ball or a box, a face seemed to appear on top of it.

Stimulating the face area produces faces, and only faces.

One man, born face-blind, has a face area that looks normal on fMRI. Having the region is not enough; its connections to the rest of the brain matter as much.

Steps

  1. Find a photo of a face you know well.
  2. Turn it upside down and study it for ten seconds.
  3. Turn it the right way up.
  4. Inverted, it looked broadly normal. Upright, any distortion stands out. Face processing is tuned to upright faces.
  5. Repeat with a photo of a house.
  6. The house is affected far less by inversion. Faces are processed as a whole configuration; most objects are not.

Time. 5 minutes

Words to know

EEG
Electroencephalography: electrodes on the scalp record the brain's electrical activity with millisecond timing.
Double dissociation
Two patients with opposite deficits. Together they show that two abilities depend on separate systems.
Intracranial recording
Electrodes placed directly on or in the brain, usually during epilepsy surgery.
