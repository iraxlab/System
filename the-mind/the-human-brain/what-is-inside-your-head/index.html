What Is Inside Your Head

What it is. Beneath the skull lie a few major structures: the folded cortex, the white-matter cables beneath it, the thalamus at the centre, the hippocampus and amygdala deep in the temporal lobes, the cerebellum at the back and the brainstem below.

What it gives you. A working map of these structures makes every later topic easier to place, and explains why damage in one location produces very specific losses.

The parts list

The outer cortex is a sheet of grey matter, folded so that a surface area roughly the size of a large pizza fits inside the skull. Beneath it, nearly half the brain's volume is white matter: long fibres wrapped in fatty myelin that link one region to another.

At the centre sits the thalamus, the relay through which vision, hearing and touch pass on their way to the cortex. Smell is the exception and reaches the cortex directly. Deep in each temporal lobe lie the hippocampus, for memory and navigation, and the amygdala, for threat.

Below and behind are the cerebellum, which refines movement, and the brainstem, which controls breathing, heart rate and the sleep-wake cycle. Damage to the brainstem is far more likely to be fatal than damage to most of the cortex.

Tap a part to meet it

  • Cortex (The outer sheet) The folded outer sheet where perception, language and reasoning take place. Unfolded, it covers roughly 2,500 square centimetres.
  • White matter (Under the sheet) Bundles of myelinated fibres connecting regions to each other. Close to half of the human brain is white matter.
  • Thalamus (The very middle) The central relay. Signals from the eyes, ears and skin stop here before continuing to their cortical areas.
  • Hippocampus (Deep inside, curled) Forms new long-term memories and maintains an internal map of places.
  • Amygdala (Deep inside, the almond) Detects threat, triggers fear responses and helps recognise fear in other people's faces.
  • Brainstem (The stalk) Controls breathing, body temperature, heart rate and arousal. Life depends on it.
  • Cerebellum (Back, underneath) Refines the timing and accuracy of movement. Its folded surface resembles a cauliflower.
  • Spinal cord (Down your back) The main cable to the body, carrying sensation up and motor commands down.
  • Smell bulb (Under the front) Receives input from the nose. Smell is the only sense that does not pass through the thalamus first.

Compact and efficient

  • 100 billion neurons in the human brain, roughly
  • 1,000s of connections on each neuron
  • 20 watts the brain's total power use, less than a household bulb
  • 2,500 cm² surface area of the unfolded cortex
  • 45% of brain volume is white matter
  • 52 cortical areas defined by microscope a century ago

Almost every sense passes through the thalamus

  • Eyes → Thalamus → Visual cortex
  • Ears → Thalamus → Auditory cortex
  • Skin → Thalamus → Somatosensory cortex
  • Nose → Olfactory cortex

Smell is the one sense with a direct route to the cortex.

Three maps every brain carries

  • Visual map Primary visual cortex holds a spatial map of the visual field. Showing people a large letter produces the same shape of activity on the cortex.
  • Body map A strip across the top of the brain maps the entire body surface. Fingertips and lips have the largest areas because they are the most sensitive.
  • Frequency map Auditory cortex arranges sounds from low to high pitch in an orderly line, called tonotopy.

What one missing part reveals

  1. H.M. had both hippocampi removed to treat epilepsy. Afterward he could form no new lasting memories.
  2. An artist and pilot lost her hippocampi to a viral infection. She remains intelligent, witty and still paints, but recalls almost no events from her life.
  3. S.M. has damage to both amygdalae. She does not experience fear and readily trusts strangers.
  4. A boy born without a cerebellum walked late but went on to manage most everyday tasks well.
  5. A woman who lost her motion area saw the world as a sequence of still frames, so a cup overflowed before she noticed it filling.

Each structure carries its own function. Lose one, and mainly that function is lost.

Three criteria for a true brain region

  • Distinct function Its neurons respond to something its neighbours do not.
  • Distinct connections It links to its own set of other regions, like a fingerprint.
  • Distinct structure Under the microscope, its layers and cell types look different.

The motion area, called MT, meets all three criteria.

Five parts of a neuron

  • Dendrites Branching inputs that receive signals from other neurons.
  • Cell body Holds the nucleus and integrates incoming signals.
  • Axon A single long fibre that carries the output signal away.
  • Myelin A fatty insulating wrap that makes the signal travel many times faster.
  • Synapse The narrow junction where a chemical message passes to the next cell.

The brain runs on about 20 watts. A well-known quiz-show computer needed around 20,000 watts to compete with human players.

Safety. Press lightly, never hard enough to hurt.

Steps

  1. Hold two pencils side by side with the tips about 5 mm apart.
  2. Close your eyes and have someone touch your fingertip with both tips at once.
  3. Repeat on the back of the hand, then on the forearm.
  4. On the fingertip you feel two points; on the forearm, one. The fingertip occupies a far larger area of the touch map in the cortex.
  5. Hum the lowest note you can, then the highest.
  6. Low and high frequencies activate different positions along the auditory cortex, laid out in order like piano keys.

Time. 10 minutes

Words to know

Thalamus
A paired structure at the centre of the brain that relays almost all sensory input to the cortex.
Hippocampus
A curved structure deep in the temporal lobe, essential for forming new memories and for spatial navigation.
Amygdala
An almond-shaped structure beside the hippocampus, central to detecting threat and processing fear.
Topographic map
An area of cortex where neighbouring points on the body, the visual field or the frequency scale are represented next to each other.
