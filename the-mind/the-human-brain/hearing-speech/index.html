Hearing Speech

What it is. From pressure waves in the air, the brain determines where a sound comes from, what produced it, what is being said and who is saying it.

What it gives you. This traces the steps from the ear to understanding a voice, and shows why speech recognition remains difficult even for advanced computers.

From air to meaning

Hearing begins with pressure waves striking the eardrum. Three small bones transmit the vibration to the cochlea, where hair cells sort it by frequency. The signal then passes through several relay stations in the brainstem before reaching the cortex.

Auditory cortex contains a map of frequency. Next to it lie regions that respond selectively to speech and, separately, to music. The speech regions respond to the sounds of language, even in a language the listener does not understand.

Speech is hard to decode because the same word sounds different from every speaker, sounds overlap in noisy rooms, and echoes smear the signal. The brain solves all three problems in real time.

Six things heard at once

  • The scene The kind of space and what is happening in it.
  • Location Where the source is, even with eyes closed.
  • The source A door, a dog, a kettle.
  • The material Wood, plastic or metal.
  • The words What is being said.
  • The speaker Who is speaking: familiar or not, young or old.

From air to cortex

  • Eardrum → Middle-ear bones → Cochlea
  • Cochlea → Brainstem relays → Frequency map
  • Frequency map → Adjacent cortex → Speech regions

Vision has one relay on its way to the cortex. Hearing has several.

Three problems the brain solves

  • Speaker variation Every voice produces a different acoustic pattern for the same word.
  • Overlapping sources Speech and music arrive mixed into one signal. Separating them is like solving x + y = 9 for both values.
  • Reverberation Echoes blur the sound, yet also reveal the size of the room.

"Bat" and "pat" differ by a delay of only about 65 milliseconds before the voice begins.

Speech-selective regions respond just as strongly to a language the listener does not understand. They process the sounds of speech, not its meaning.

Steps

  1. Sit with your eyes closed while someone moves around the room, speaking.
  2. Point to the voice each time it changes position.
  3. You locate it accurately without sight, using tiny differences in timing and loudness between your ears.
  4. Ask them to drop a spoon, a pencil and a plastic cup on a table.
  5. You identify the material of each object from sound alone.

Time. 5 minutes

Words to know

Cochlea
The spiral structure of the inner ear that separates sound by frequency.
Spectrogram
A visual display of sound, with frequency on the vertical axis and time on the horizontal.
Phoneme
The smallest unit of sound that changes meaning, such as the difference between "bat" and "pat".
