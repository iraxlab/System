Testing an Idea

What it is. A sound experiment changes one variable, measures one outcome and rules out every other explanation for the result.

What it gives you. Understanding good design lets you test claims yourself and recognise weak ones, whether in research, in the media or in your own habits.

One change, one measure

An experiment compares conditions that differ in exactly one respect. If the outcome differs, that one factor is the cause. The difficulty lies in making sure nothing else differs.

Suppose a region responds more to faces than to objects. Faces are also more rounded, more interesting and more familiar. Any of these could be the real cause. Each must be ruled out with a carefully chosen comparison.

Brain scans show only that a region is active. To show it is necessary, the region must be disrupted. TMS does this safely and briefly in healthy volunteers.

Six elements of a sound experiment

  • Independent variable The single factor you change on purpose.
  • Dependent variable The outcome you measure, such as accuracy or reaction time.
  • Prediction A specific statement of what you will observe if the hypothesis is correct.
  • Confound Faces are also curvier and more engaging than objects. That difference could be the true cause.
  • Control Compare snakes with coiled hoses: the same shape, without the snake.
  • Baseline A condition as close to "off" as possible, such as fixating a dot.

To prove a cause, change the cause

  1. A scan shows a region becoming active. It does not prove the region is required.
  2. TMS delivers a magnetic pulse lasting less than a millisecond over a chosen region.
  3. Over a face-selective area near the surface, it lowers face-matching accuracy from 85 to 78 percent.
  4. The effect appears only when the pulse arrives 60 to 100 milliseconds after the face.
  5. In monkeys, six face patches are wired together and act as one coordinated network.

TMS is not used on people with a history of seizures.

Caffeine increases the fMRI signal. Some labs control for this by giving every volunteer the same dose before scanning.

Steps

  1. Choose a question, such as whether background music helps you remember a word list.
  2. Write down the one variable you change (music on or off) and the one outcome you measure (words recalled).
  3. List three other differences that could creep in: time of day, fatigue, the specific list. Hold them constant.
  4. Run both conditions in alternating order over four days.
  5. Compare the averages. With only one variable changed, only that variable can explain the difference.

Time. 4 days, 10 minutes a day

Words to know

Confound
An unintended second difference between conditions that could explain the result instead of the variable you changed.
Control condition
A condition identical to the test condition except for the one factor being studied.
TMS
Transcranial magnetic stimulation: a magnetic pulse through the scalp that briefly disrupts the cortex beneath it.
