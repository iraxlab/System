Attention and Awareness

What it is. Attention selects a small fraction of sensory input for awareness and filters out the rest.

What it gives you. This reveals how much of the visual world goes unnoticed, and how deliberate attention shapes what we experience.

A limited, selective filter

The senses deliver far more information than the brain can fully process. Attention selects what reaches awareness. The result is that people often miss large, obvious changes when their attention is directed elsewhere.

Attention can move with the eyes, which jump two to four times per second, or independently of them. Even while fixating one point, it is possible to attend to an object in peripheral vision.

In the brain, attention increases responses in the regions processing the attended item. Frontal and parietal regions control where attention goes. Damage to them can leave a person able to see only one object at a time.

How much goes unseen

  1. A street scene flashes repeatedly while awnings, colours and a car change. Most viewers miss most changes.
  2. In a short film, the set changes between every shot. Viewers notice almost none of it.
  3. Trained pilots in a flight simulator often landed straight through an aircraft parked on the runway.
  4. Afterward, many refused to believe it had been there.
  5. A hands-free phone call still divides limited attention while driving.

Attention is a filter: limited in capacity and directed by choice.

Overt and covert attention

  • Overt attention The eyes move two to four times per second. The central fovea occupies a disproportionately large share of visual cortex.
  • Covert attention With the eyes fixed on one point, attention can still shift to an object in the periphery.
  • Feature selection Attending to red letters among blue ones filters out the blue at little cost.

What attention changes

  • Amplification The face area responds far more strongly when faces are attended.
  • Early filtering Effects appear even in the thalamus, the first relay after the eye.
  • Control network Frontal and parietal regions direct where attention goes.
  • Bilateral damage Some patients can perceive only one object at a time.
  • Rivalry A face shown to one eye and a house to the other: awareness alternates while the input stays the same.
  • Unseen processing A scene that is not consciously seen still produces a small response in the place area.

A pike catches its first fish faster when one stickleback is in the tank than when there are ten. Too many targets split attention, even in fish.

Steps

  1. Find a pair of "spot the difference" images.
  2. Alternate between them once per second without searching.
  3. Many changes remain invisible. This is change blindness.
  4. Now examine one small region at a time.
  5. The differences appear. Awareness follows attention.
  6. Fix your eyes on someone's nose while they hold fingers out to the side. Count them without moving your eyes.

Time. 5 minutes

Words to know

Selective attention
Prioritising some information for processing while suppressing the rest.
Change blindness
Failure to notice large changes in a scene when attention is elsewhere.
Binocular rivalry
When each eye sees a different image, awareness alternates between them while the input stays constant.
