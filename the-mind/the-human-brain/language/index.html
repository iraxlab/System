Language

What it is. Language transfers a thought from one mind to another. Brain evidence shows it is distinct from thought itself.

What it gives you. This clarifies what language contributes to thinking, and what the mind can do without it.

Language is not thought

Language has several layers: sounds or signs, words and their meanings, grammar that combines them, and the speaker's intent. A network of regions, mainly in the left frontal and temporal lobes, handles them.

This network is highly selective. It responds to sentences but not to arithmetic, logic or music. People who lose almost all language after a stroke can still reason, calculate and solve puzzles.

Language is therefore a system for communicating thought, not the medium of thought itself. It does, however, play a major role in how children learn from others.

Three layers of language

  • Sounds and words Speech sounds, or hand shapes in sign languages, and the concepts each word refers to.
  • Grammar Rules that assign roles: "the shark bites the man" is not "the man bites the shark".
  • Intent "It would be great if you could pass the salt" is a request, not an observation.

Evidence from people

  1. Infants of three to six months show rich reasoning before acquiring any words.
  2. The Pirahã of the Amazon have no exact number words, yet match quantities by sight.
  3. In 1861, a patient who could say only one syllable still showed clear understanding and thought.
  4. People who lose nearly all language after stroke still solve arithmetic, logic problems and puzzles, and enjoy music.
  5. An art critic losing language to a tumour wrote that his thinking remained broad and intact.

Language is not thought. Children, however, rely on it to learn from others.

A dog with a large vocabulary

  • 1,000 names A Border Collie learned a distinct name for each of her toys.
  • Inference Asked for an unfamiliar name, she chose the one toy she had not seen before.
  • No grammar She could not follow sentences that specify who did what to whom.

Humans combine words to express ideas that have never been expressed before.

In some Aboriginal Australian languages, a greeting asks which direction you are heading, and an insect is described as being on your south-east leg.

Steps

  1. Say "the shark bites the man", then "the man bites the shark".
  2. Same words, opposite meaning. Word order carries it.
  3. Close your eyes and point south. Then check.
  4. Speakers of some Aboriginal Australian languages always know, because their language requires compass directions in everyday speech.

Time. 5 minutes

Words to know

Syntax
The rules governing how words combine into sentences.
Aphasia
Loss or impairment of language after brain damage, usually to the left hemisphere.
Pragmatics
The intended meaning of an utterance, beyond its literal words.
