Other Minds

What it is. Humans infer what others see, want and believe. One region, near the junction of the right temporal and parietal lobes, responds specifically to thoughts about beliefs.

What it gives you. This explains how we understand and predict one another, why the ability takes years to mature, and how it shapes moral judgement.

Reading minds

Understanding behaviour depends on inferring hidden mental states. Romeo reaches for the poison because he believes Juliet is dead. Without his belief, the action makes no sense.

Children pass simple false-belief tests around age four or five, and continue to refine the skill for years. Very young children already infer goals and offer help, but understanding that others can hold mistaken beliefs comes later.

In adults, the right temporo-parietal junction responds strongly when people consider another person's beliefs, but not their appearance, hunger or pain. Disrupting it changes how people judge accidental harm.

Two pirates, two sandwiches

  1. One pirate places his sandwich on a chest and leaves for a drink.
  2. The wind blows it onto the grass. A second pirate places his own sandwich on the chest.
  3. A five-year-old predicts the first pirate will think the remaining sandwich is his.
  4. A three-year-old predicts he will take his own, explaining that it was dirty.
  5. Only around age seven do children say the blame belongs to the wind.

Understanding that others can hold false beliefs takes years to develop fully.

Hidden, but essential

  • Perception What another person has seen, and what they have missed.
  • Desire What they want. Romeo reaches for the poison because he wants to die.
  • Belief What they hold to be true. He believes Juliet is dead; she is not.

What drives its response

  • Another person's thoughts Strong response.
  • Their appearance Little response.
  • Their hunger or pain Little response.
  • A silent animated film Strong at moments when characters form beliefs.
  • Development Becomes more selective through childhood after age four.
  • Disrupted with TMS Accidental harm is judged more harshly.

Whether a person believed a powder was sugar or poison changes how we judge the same outcome.

At 18 months, toddlers spontaneously help an adult whose hands are full, without being asked or rewarded.

Steps

  1. Take two cups, a coin and a small figure.
  2. While the figure "watches", hide the coin under cup A.
  3. Move the figure out of view, then move the coin to cup B.
  4. Ask a child aged 3 to 7 where the figure will look.
  5. Most five-year-olds say A, because the figure did not see the move. Most three-year-olds say B.

Time. 5 minutes

Words to know

Theory of mind
The ability to attribute thoughts, beliefs and intentions to others.
False belief
A belief held by someone that no longer matches reality.
Right TPJ
The right temporo-parietal junction, a region that responds selectively when we think about other people's thoughts.
