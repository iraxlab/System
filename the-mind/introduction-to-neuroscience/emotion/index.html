Emotion

What it is. Emotion involves bodily responses, conscious feelings and behaviour, coordinated by circuits that include the amygdala, hypothalamus and prefrontal cortex.

What it gives you. These circuits explain fear learning, aggression and how emotion shapes decisions, and they underlie anxiety and mood disorders.

Feeling and expression

Emotion has two sides. Emotional expression is what can be observed: changes in heart rate, posture, facial expression and behaviour. Emotional experience is the conscious feeling. Animal studies can measure the first; only humans can report the second.

Early theories disagreed about order. One held that we feel afraid because the body reacts. Another held that brain and body react at the same time, independently. Modern evidence supports a mix: bodily signals shape feelings, but are not the whole story.

There is no single emotion centre. Different emotions rely on overlapping circuits. The amygdala is most closely tied to fear, while the prefrontal cortex helps interpret and regulate emotional responses.

What comes first?

  • James-Lange We feel afraid because we notice our body trembling and our heart racing. Feeling follows the body.
  • Cannon-Bard The brain triggers feeling and bodily change at the same time. Emotions remain after the body's signals are cut.
  • Unconscious emotion Studies show that faces flashed too briefly to see still activate the amygdala and change behaviour.

A history of emotion circuits

  1. Broca named the limbic lobe, a ring of cortex around the brainstem, in 1878.
  2. Papez proposed in 1937 a loop through the hypothalamus, thalamus, cingulate cortex and hippocampus.
  3. In 1848, Phineas Gage survived an iron rod through his frontal lobe. His personality changed sharply.
  4. Monkeys with temporal lobe removal lost fear and showed odd behaviour: the Klüver-Bucy syndrome.
  5. Later work showed the hippocampus is mainly for memory, and the amygdala for emotion.

The term limbic system survives, but its boundaries are debated.

A circuit for learned fear

  • Input Receives information from every sense, both directly from the thalamus and from the cortex.
  • Fast route A direct thalamic path gives a quick, rough signal, enough to trigger a startle.
  • Slow route A cortical path delivers detail, allowing a more accurate judgement.
  • Output Projections to the hypothalamus and brainstem raise heart rate, cortisol and freezing.
  • Learning When a tone predicts a shock, synapses in the amygdala strengthen and the tone alone produces fear.
  • Human damage People with amygdala damage struggle to recognise fear in faces and show weak fear conditioning.

Circuits and chemistry

  • Hypothalamus Stimulating different areas produces either defensive rage or calm predatory attack in animals.
  • Serotonin Low serotonin activity is linked to impulsive aggression in animals and humans.
  • Surgery Amygdala and frontal lobe surgery were once used to reduce aggression. Frontal lobotomy was abandoned for its severe effects.

A patient known as S.M., with damage to both amygdalae, shows almost no fear. She approached snakes and spiders with curiosity and reported no fear during life-threatening events.

Steps

  1. Recall a moment when something startled you.
  2. Notice which came first: the jump or the feeling of fear.
  3. The body often reacts before conscious fear. A fast route through the amygdala acts within milliseconds.
  4. Notice how quickly you then judged the situation as safe or not.
  5. A slower route through the cortex assesses detail and can calm the response.
  6. Notice any change in your breath or heartbeat.
  7. Bodily changes feed back to the brain and shape the feeling itself.

Time. 2 minutes

Words to know

Amygdala
An almond-shaped structure in the temporal lobe central to fear and emotional learning.
Limbic system
An older term for a group of structures linked to emotion, including the amygdala and hippocampus.
Fear conditioning
Learning that a neutral cue predicts something harmful, so the cue alone produces fear.
