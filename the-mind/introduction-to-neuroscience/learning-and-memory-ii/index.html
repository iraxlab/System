Learning and Memory II

What it is. Memories are stored as changes in the strength of synapses. Long-term strengthening (LTP) and weakening (LTD) of synapses, followed by protein synthesis and structural growth, form the cellular basis of learning.

What it gives you. These mechanisms explain why repetition, spacing and sleep improve learning, and they are the targets of research on memory disorders.

Memory written into synapses

Hebb predicted in 1949 that memory depends on changes in the strength of connections between neurons. Decades later, experiments confirmed it. A memory is not stored in a single cell but in the pattern of strengthened and weakened synapses across many cells.

Simple animals made the first breakthroughs. The sea slug Aplysia has large, identifiable neurons and simple reflexes that can be trained. Studies of these reflexes showed how learning changes transmitter release at specific synapses.

In mammals, the hippocampus and cerebellum revealed two complementary mechanisms: LTP, which strengthens synapses, and LTD, which weakens them. Both are needed. A system that only strengthens would quickly saturate.

Nonassociative and associative

  • Habituation A repeated, harmless stimulus produces a smaller and smaller response. You stop noticing the hum of a fridge.
  • Sensitization A strong or painful stimulus increases responses to other stimuli for a period afterward.
  • Conditioning Classical conditioning links two stimuli; instrumental conditioning links an action to its outcome.

Learning in a sea slug

  1. Touching the slug's siphon makes it withdraw its gill, a simple protective reflex.
  2. Repeated gentle touch reduces transmitter release from sensory neurons: habituation.
  3. A shock to the tail releases serotonin, which increases transmitter release: sensitization.
  4. With repeated training, new proteins are made and new synapses grow.
  5. Eric Kandel led this work and shared the Nobel Prize in 2000.

Short-term memory changes existing proteins. Long-term memory needs new ones.

How synapses strengthen

  • Trigger Strong, high-frequency input to hippocampal neurons produces lasting strengthening of their synapses.
  • Coincidence NMDA receptors open only when glutamate binds and the cell is already active, matching Hebb's rule.
  • Calcium Calcium entering through NMDA receptors activates enzymes, especially CaMKII.
  • More receptors CaMKII adds AMPA receptors to the synapse, making it more responsive to glutamate.
  • Timing If input arrives just before the cell fires, the synapse strengthens; if just after, it weakens.
  • Link to memory Blocking NMDA receptors in rats prevents learning a water maze.

From minutes to a lifetime

  • Protein synthesis Drugs that block protein synthesis just after learning prevent long-term, but not short-term, memory.
  • CREB This switch protein activates genes for consolidation. Raising it in animals improves memory.
  • Structural change Learning adds and enlarges dendritic spines, physically reshaping connections.

LTD in the cerebellum weakens specific synapses and supports motor learning, such as adapting a reflex.

Molecular mechanisms

  • 1973 LTP first described in the rabbit hippocampus
  • 2000 Nobel Prize to Kandel
  • 20,000 neurons in the Aplysia nervous system
  • 20 ms timing window for strengthening or weakening
  • hours for protein synthesis to consolidate a memory
  • 2 directions of change: LTP and LTD

Mice engineered with extra NMDA receptor subunits learn some tasks faster. They were nicknamed Doogie mice, after a fictional teenage doctor.

Steps

  1. Recall something you learned only once, then something you practised many times.
  2. Compare how clearly each returns.
  3. Repetition strengthens the same synapses again and again, making the trace durable.
  4. Recall a fact learned the night before a good sleep.
  5. Consolidation depends on new proteins, which are made in the hours after learning.
  6. Notice a habit you have stopped noticing, such as a ticking clock.
  7. That is habituation: a simple form of learning based on reduced transmitter release.

Time. 10 minutes

Words to know

LTP
A lasting increase in synaptic strength after strong, repeated activity. Often called long-term strengthening.
NMDA receptor
A glutamate receptor that opens only when the cell is already active, detecting coincident input.
CREB
A protein that switches on genes needed to turn short-term changes into long-term memory.
