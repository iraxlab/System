Neurons and Their Helpers

What it is. A neuron has a cell body, branching dendrites that receive input and a single axon that sends output. Glial cells surround it and supply, insulate, clean and protect.

What it gives you. Every later topic depends on these parts. The shape of a neuron also tells you a great deal about its role in a circuit.

The cell that does the thinking

The brain holds roughly 100 billion neurons and at least as many glial cells. A neuron shares the basic equipment of any cell: a nucleus, mitochondria for fuel and ribosomes for building proteins. What sets it apart is shape.

Dendrites form a branching tree that collects input from thousands of other cells. The axon is a single cable, even in width, that carries the output. Where an axon ends on another cell, it forms a synapse.

Glia were long seen as packing material. They are now known to regulate the chemical environment, insulate axons, remove debris and shape how synapses form and work.

A century of staining and argument

  1. Brain tissue is soft and colourless. Fixatives were needed to harden it and thin slices to see through it.
  2. The Nissl stain marks cell bodies, which made the layered structure of the cortex visible for the first time.
  3. Golgi found a silver stain that fills a small number of cells completely, showing every branch.
  4. Cajal used Golgi's method to argue that neurons are separate cells that touch rather than fuse.
  5. Both shared the Nobel Prize in 1906. The electron microscope later confirmed that Cajal was right.

Golgi held to the opposite view, a continuous network, until the end.

The working parts

  • Nucleus Holds the DNA. Genes are copied into messenger RNA, which leaves the nucleus to be read.
  • Rough ER Stacks of membrane studded with ribosomes, where most proteins are made. Neurons have large amounts of it.
  • Mitochondria Convert glucose and oxygen into ATP, the cell's fuel. Synapses are especially rich in them.
  • Axon hillock The base of the axon, where the decision to fire is made. Its membrane carries dense ion channels.
  • Axon terminal The end of the axon, packed with vesicles of transmitter ready to release onto the next cell.
  • Dendrites The input tree. Their surface is covered with receptors for transmitters released by other neurons.

Classified by shape, length and chemistry

  • By number of branches Unipolar, bipolar and multipolar neurons have one, two or many processes. Most brain neurons are multipolar.
  • By dendrite shape Pyramidal cells in the cortex have a triangular body; stellate cells are star-shaped.
  • By spines Spiny neurons tend to be excitatory. Smooth, spineless neurons are usually inhibitory.
  • By connection Sensory neurons receive from the senses, motor neurons drive muscles and interneurons connect neurons to each other.
  • By axon length Projection neurons send long axons to other regions; local circuit neurons stay within one area.
  • By transmitter Cells that release acetylcholine are called cholinergic; those that release dopamine, dopaminergic.

The support staff

  • Astrocytes Fill the space between neurons, absorb excess transmitter and control the ion balance that neurons depend on.
  • Myelinating glia Oligodendrocytes in the brain and Schwann cells in the body wrap axons in myelin. Gaps between wraps are the nodes of Ranvier.
  • Microglia The brain's immune cells. They remove debris from dead cells and are increasingly linked to synapse pruning.

Ependymal cells also line the fluid-filled ventricles and help guide cell migration during development.

The scale of the cell

  • 100 billion neurons in the human brain, roughly
  • 1,000 mm per day: fastest transport rate along an axon
  • 0.01 to 0.05 mm diameter of a typical neuron cell body
  • 1906 Golgi and Cajal share the Nobel Prize
  • 10x more glia than neurons, by older estimates
  • 1 metre+ length of the longest axons in the body

Animals raised in enriched environments, with toys and company, grow more dendritic spines. In people with intellectual disability such as fragile X syndrome, spines are often long, thin and immature.

Steps

  1. Hold one hand up with the fingers spread.
  2. Read the fingers as dendrites, the palm as the cell body and the forearm as the axon.
  3. Slide a coin from the palm to the elbow.
  4. This models anterograde transport: proteins carried outward along microtubules at up to 1,000 mm a day.
  5. Slide the coin back to the palm.
  6. This models retrograde transport. Herpes and rabies viruses use the same return route to reach the cell body.

Time. 3 minutes

Words to know

Axon
The single output fibre of a neuron. In the body it can exceed one metre in length.
Dendritic spine
A small protrusion on a dendrite that receives one synaptic input and changes shape with experience.
Myelin
Layers of glial membrane wrapped around an axon that make conduction many times faster.
