Sleep

What it is. Sleep is an active, regulated state with two main types, non-REM and REM, cycling through the night. A brain clock in the hypothalamus times sleep and waking to the 24-hour day.

What it gives you. Sleep is essential for health, memory and mood. Understanding it helps explain jet lag, insomnia and disorders such as narcolepsy.

The brain at night

The Earth turns every 24 hours, and nearly every living thing keeps time with it. In humans, the most visible rhythm is sleep and waking. Sleep is not a switching-off. It is a structured state with its own patterns of brain activity.

Brain rhythms are recorded with the EEG. Waking produces fast, irregular, low-amplitude waves. Deep non-REM sleep produces slow, large waves as millions of neurons fire in synchrony. REM sleep looks almost like waking on the EEG, yet the body is paralysed.

Two systems control sleep. A homeostatic drive builds with time awake, partly through adenosine. A circadian clock in the hypothalamus sets the best window for sleep each day. Sleep comes most easily when both align.

What the EEG shows

  • Beta Fast waves above 14 per second, seen during alert thinking and attention.
  • Alpha 8 to 13 per second, seen during relaxed wakefulness with eyes closed.
  • Theta 4 to 7 per second, seen in drowsiness and some stages of sleep.
  • Delta Slow waves below 4 per second, dominant in deep non-REM sleep.
  • Synchrony Large waves mean many neurons fire together, often paced by the thalamus.
  • Epilepsy Seizures are bursts of excessive synchronous activity, visible as spikes on the EEG.

One night, step by step

  1. Stage 1: light sleep for a few minutes, slow rolling eye movements.
  2. Stage 2: sleep spindles and K-complexes appear. About half the night is spent here.
  3. Stages 3 and 4: deep slow-wave sleep. Hardest to wake from; most common early in the night.
  4. REM: rapid eye movements, vivid dreams and paralysis of most muscles.
  5. One cycle takes about 90 minutes. REM periods lengthen toward morning.

Sleepwalking and night terrors occur in deep non-REM sleep, not during dreams.

Two main theories

  • Restoration Sleep allows the brain and body to recover. Deprived animals lose weight, temperature control and eventually die.
  • Adaptation Sleep keeps animals still and safe during hours when activity would be dangerous or wasteful.
  • Memory Both slow-wave and REM sleep support consolidation of the day's learning.

A high school student stayed awake for 11 days in 1964. He recovered fully after sleeping, but showed lapses and irritability throughout.

What switches sleep on and off

  • Arousal system Brainstem and forebrain neurons using norepinephrine, serotonin, acetylcholine and histamine keep the cortex awake.
  • Sleep switch A group of hypothalamic neurons inhibits the arousal system to start sleep.
  • REM control Acetylcholine neurons in the brainstem trigger REM; the same region blocks motor neurons, paralysing the body.
  • Adenosine Builds up during waking and promotes sleep. Caffeine blocks its receptors.
  • Narcolepsy Loss of orexin neurons causes sudden sleep attacks and collapse during strong emotion.
  • Antihistamines Older types cross into the brain and block histamine, which is why they cause drowsiness.

Circadian rhythms

  • The clock The suprachiasmatic nucleus contains about 20,000 neurons. Each cell keeps time through a loop of clock genes.
  • Free running Without daylight, the human clock drifts to a cycle slightly longer than 24 hours.
  • Light input Light-sensitive ganglion cells with melanopsin reset the clock daily, even in some blind people.

Sleep

  • 90 min length of one sleep cycle
  • 20-25% of adult sleep spent in REM
  • 20,000 neurons in the suprachiasmatic nucleus
  • 11 days longest documented voluntary sleep deprivation
  • 24.2 h average human free-running cycle
  • 4-6 sleep cycles in a normal night

A hamster with a single clock gene mutation ran on a 20-hour cycle. Transplanting its suprachiasmatic nucleus into a normal hamster gave the host the donor's rhythm.

Safety. Persistent poor sleep, loud snoring or sudden daytime sleep should be checked by a doctor.

Steps

  1. For one week, note your bedtime and the time you wake naturally.
  2. Notice when you feel most alert and when you feel drowsy.
  3. The suprachiasmatic nucleus sets a daily rhythm of alertness, hormone release and temperature.
  4. Get bright light within an hour of waking.
  5. Morning light resets the clock through special light-sensitive cells in the retina.
  6. Keep the last two hours before bed dim.
  7. Darkness allows melatonin to rise, which signals night to the body.

Time. 7 days

Words to know

EEG
Electroencephalogram: a recording of brain electrical rhythms from electrodes on the scalp.
REM sleep
Rapid eye movement sleep: an active brain state with vivid dreams and paralysed muscles.
Suprachiasmatic nucleus
A small cluster of cells in the hypothalamus that acts as the brain's master clock.
