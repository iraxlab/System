The Spike

What it is. A spike is a brief reversal of the membrane charge, about one millisecond long, that travels down the axon without fading. It is the neuron's basic unit of information.

What it gives you. Spikes are how the nervous system transmits every sensation, thought and command over distance. Their timing and rate carry the message.

All or none

A spike either happens fully or not at all. Once the membrane reaches threshold, the same shape and size of signal follows every time. Stronger input does not make a bigger spike; it makes more spikes per second.

The mechanism was worked out in the 1950s using the giant axon of the squid, about a millimetre wide. Later, the patch-clamp method allowed scientists to record the current through a single channel protein.

Speed depends on axon width and insulation. Thin bare axons conduct at about one metre per second. Thick myelinated axons reach over one hundred, fast enough to coordinate a whole body in real time.

One spike, start to finish

  1. Rising phase: sodium channels open and sodium floods in. The inside swings positive.
  2. Overshoot: the charge peaks near plus 40 millivolts, close to the sodium equilibrium.
  3. Falling phase: sodium channels inactivate and potassium channels open. Charge drops.
  4. Undershoot: potassium keeps flowing briefly, taking the cell below its resting value.
  5. Recovery: channels reset and the membrane returns to rest, ready for the next spike.

The whole cycle takes about two milliseconds.

How spikes carry information

  • Rate A stronger stimulus produces a higher firing rate, up to about 1,000 spikes per second in some cells.
  • Timing The exact pattern and timing of spikes can carry additional information beyond rate.
  • Direction The refractory period prevents a spike from travelling backward, so signals move one way along the axon.

From squid to single molecules

  1. Hodgkin and Huxley recorded from the squid giant axon in the 1940s and 1950s.
  2. They used a voltage clamp to hold the membrane at a set value and measure ion currents.
  3. Their mathematical model predicted the spike shape. They won the Nobel Prize in 1963.
  4. Neher and Sakmann developed the patch clamp to record from single channels.
  5. Gene cloning then revealed the structure of the sodium channel protein itself.

The squid axon is large because it drives the animal's fast escape jet.

Two ways to speed a signal

  • Wider axon A thicker axon offers less internal resistance, so current spreads farther and faster.
  • Myelin Insulating wraps stop current leaking out between gaps, so the signal jumps from node to node.
  • Nodes of Ranvier Short bare gaps packed with sodium channels, where the spike is renewed about every millimetre.

Natural poisons that target channels

  • Tetrodotoxin Found in puffer fish. It plugs sodium channels and can stop breathing within hours.
  • Saxitoxin Made by algae in red tides and concentrated in shellfish. It also blocks sodium channels.
  • Batrachotoxin From a Colombian frog. It holds sodium channels open, so neurons cannot reset.
  • Local anaesthetics Lidocaine and similar drugs block sodium channels in pain fibres, stopping signals reaching the brain.
  • Scorpion toxins Some slow sodium channel inactivation and extend each spike.
  • Medical use Each toxin binds one site, which made them essential tools for mapping channel structure.

Diseases of myelin

  • Multiple sclerosis The immune system attacks myelin in the brain and spinal cord, slowing or blocking signals.
  • Symptoms Weakness, numbness, visual loss and poor coordination, depending on which tracts are affected.
  • Peripheral loss Guillain-Barré syndrome attacks the myelin of nerves in the body, often after an infection.

If the 100 billion neurons of one brain were laid out end to end with their axons, the total length would wrap the Earth several times.

Steps

  1. Picture the axon at rest, minus 65 millivolts inside.
  2. Push the charge up to the threshold, about minus 50.
  3. Voltage-gated sodium channels open. Sodium rushes in and the inside turns positive within half a millisecond.
  4. Hold for a moment.
  5. Sodium channels inactivate and potassium channels open. Potassium leaves and the charge falls back.
  6. Note the brief overshoot below the resting value.
  7. During this refractory period, a new spike is hard or impossible to trigger. This keeps the signal moving in one direction.

Time. 5 minutes

Words to know

Threshold
The voltage at which enough sodium channels open to trigger a full spike.
Refractory period
A short window after a spike when the neuron cannot fire again, or needs a much stronger input.
Saltatory conduction
The way a spike jumps from one gap in the myelin to the next, which greatly increases speed.
