From Eye to Brain

What it is. Signals from each eye travel through the optic chiasm and thalamus to the primary visual cortex at the back of the brain, where the image is broken down into edges, orientations and colours.

What it gives you. This pathway explains why damage in different places causes very different kinds of visual loss, and how the brain begins to build a picture from simple parts.

The route from retina to cortex

The optic nerves meet under the brain at the chiasm. Fibres from the half of each retina nearest the nose cross over; the rest stay on the same side. As a result, each half of the brain receives the opposite half of the visual world from both eyes.

About 90 percent of the fibres go to the lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus. The rest go to the hypothalamus for daily rhythms, the pretectum for pupil control and the superior colliculus for eye movements.

From the thalamus, signals reach primary visual cortex, also called V1 or striate cortex. Here, cells stop responding to spots of light and start responding to lines and edges at particular angles.

Lesions and what they cause

  • Optic nerve Damage to one nerve causes blindness in that eye only.
  • Optic chiasm Pressure here, often from a pituitary tumour, removes the outer visual field on both sides, leaving tunnel vision.
  • Optic tract Damage behind the chiasm removes the opposite half of the visual field in both eyes.

A neurologist can often locate a lesion from the pattern of visual loss alone.

Lateral geniculate nucleus

  • Six layers Stacked like bent pancakes. Each layer receives from one eye only.
  • Eye segregation Layers 1, 4 and 6 receive from the opposite eye; layers 2, 3 and 5 from the same-side eye.
  • Magno layers The two bottom layers receive from M-type ganglion cells: motion and coarse form.
  • Parvo layers The four top layers receive from P-type cells: fine detail and red-green colour.
  • Konio layers Thin layers between the others carry blue-yellow colour information.
  • Feedback About 80 percent of its excitatory input comes back from the cortex, not the eye.

Building blocks of the image

  1. V1 lies at the back of the occipital lobe and holds a map of the visual field, with the centre greatly enlarged.
  2. It is about 2 mm thick and arranged in six main layers. Input from the thalamus arrives in layer IV.
  3. Columns of cells prefer input from one eye or the other, in alternating stripes about half a millimetre wide.
  4. Neighbouring columns prefer edges at gradually shifting angles, forming a full 180 degree cycle.
  5. Patches rich in the enzyme cytochrome oxidase, called blobs, handle colour.

Hubel and Wiesel mapped this structure in the 1960s and 70s and shared the Nobel Prize in 1981.

From spots to edges

  • Simple cells Respond to an edge at one angle and one exact position, built from lined-up thalamic inputs.
  • Complex cells Respond to an edge at one angle anywhere within a wider area. Many also prefer one direction of motion.
  • Binocular cells Above layer IV, most cells receive from both eyes, which is the basis of depth perception.

The central visual pathway

  • 90% of optic nerve fibres go to the thalamus
  • 10% go to the superior colliculus for eye movements
  • 6 layers in the lateral geniculate nucleus
  • 2 mm thickness of primary visual cortex
  • 0.5 mm width of one ocular dominance column
  • 1981 Nobel Prize for Hubel and Wiesel

Orientation selectivity was found by accident. Hubel and Wiesel saw a cell fire strongly as they slid a glass slide into their projector, casting a faint straight edge across the screen.

Steps

  1. Look straight ahead and hold your arms out to the sides.
  2. Wiggle the fingers of your left hand while keeping your eyes fixed forward.
  3. Everything in your left visual field is processed by the right hemisphere, and the reverse.
  4. Cover one eye, then the other, while looking at the same point.
  5. Each eye sees most of both fields. Fibres from the inner half of each retina cross at the chiasm.
  6. Look at a vertical line, then a tilted one.
  7. Different groups of cells in primary visual cortex respond to each orientation.

Time. 2 minutes

Words to know

Optic chiasm
The crossing point under the brain where fibres from the inner half of each retina switch sides.
LGN
The lateral geniculate nucleus: the thalamic relay between retina and visual cortex.
Orientation selectivity
The property of a visual cortex cell that fires most to an edge at one particular angle.
