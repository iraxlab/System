Hearing

What it is. The ear converts pressure waves in air into fluid waves in the cochlea, where hair cells turn vibration into electrical signals. A separate set of organs in the inner ear senses balance and head motion.

What it gives you. Hearing supports speech and music, and the balance system keeps vision stable while the head moves. Damage to either is common and often preventable.

From air to fluid to nerve

Sound is a pattern of pressure waves in air. Frequency sets pitch and amplitude sets loudness. Humans hear roughly 20 to 20,000 cycles per second, with the range narrowing at the high end with age.

The outer ear funnels sound to the eardrum. Three small bones in the middle ear pass the vibration to the oval window of the cochlea. Because the eardrum is much larger than the window, pressure is concentrated about twenty times, overcoming the resistance of fluid.

Inside the cochlea, the basilar membrane vibrates at different points for different frequencies. Hair cells along it convert that motion into electrical signals, sent through the auditory nerve to the brainstem.

Outer, middle and inner ear

  • Pinna The visible outer ear. Its folds alter sound slightly depending on whether it comes from above or below.
  • Eardrum A thin membrane that vibrates with incoming pressure waves.
  • Ossicles Malleus, incus and stapes: the three smallest bones in the body, linked as a lever system.
  • Attenuation reflex Muscles stiffen the ossicles in response to loud sounds, protecting the inner ear from sustained noise.
  • Cochlea A coiled tube of three fluid chambers. Unrolled, it is about 32 mm long.
  • Eustachian tube Connects the middle ear to the throat and equalises pressure when you swallow.

The ear's sensors

  • Inner hair cells About 3,500 per ear in a single row. They send nearly all the information to the brain.
  • Outer hair cells About 12,000 in three rows. They change length with each vibration, amplifying the motion of the membrane.
  • Transduction Bending the hairs pulls tiny linking filaments that open channels directly, faster than any chemical cascade.

Outer hair cells are so active that the ear can emit faint sounds of its own, used to screen newborn hearing.

How the brain finds a source

  1. For low frequencies, the brain compares arrival times. A sound from the left reaches the left ear up to 0.6 ms earlier.
  2. For high frequencies, the head casts a sound shadow, so the far ear hears it more quietly.
  3. Neurons in the superior olive in the brainstem compare input from both ears.
  4. For height, the brain reads how the pinna reshapes the sound.
  5. The result is a map of sound location used to orient the eyes and head.

A one-ear injury makes horizontal localisation much harder.

The vestibular system

  • Otolith organs The utricle and saccule contain small calcium crystals that sense gravity and straight-line acceleration.
  • Semicircular canals Three fluid-filled loops at right angles to each other sense rotation of the head in any direction.
  • Vestibulo-ocular reflex Moves the eyes opposite to head motion within about 10 ms, keeping the image steady.

Disorders and treatments

  • Conductive loss Sound cannot reach the cochlea, often from fluid, wax or stiff ossicles. Usually treatable.
  • Sensorineural loss Damage to hair cells or the auditory nerve, often from noise or ageing. Usually permanent.
  • Cochlear implant Electrodes inserted along the cochlea stimulate the nerve directly, bypassing lost hair cells.

Tinnitus, a ringing without sound, often follows hair cell damage.

The ear

  • 20-20,000 Hz range of human hearing
  • 20x pressure gain from eardrum to oval window
  • 15,500 hair cells in each cochlea
  • 0.6 ms largest arrival time difference between ears
  • 85 dB level at which long exposure starts to cause damage
  • 3 semicircular canals in each ear

Elephants communicate with infrasound below 20 Hz that travels several kilometres. Bats and dolphins navigate with ultrasound above 100,000 Hz, far beyond the human range.

Safety. Prolonged exposure above 85 decibels damages hair cells permanently. Keep headphone volume moderate.

Steps

  1. Sit in a quiet room and close your eyes.
  2. Snap your fingers to the left of your head, then the right.
  3. You locate the sound from tiny differences in arrival time and loudness between your two ears.
  4. Hum a low note, then a high one.
  5. Low frequencies vibrate the far end of the cochlea; high frequencies vibrate the near end.
  6. Shake your head side to side while reading a line of text.
  7. The text stays readable. The vestibular system moves your eyes against your head automatically.

Time. 5 minutes

Words to know

Cochlea
The snail-shaped, fluid-filled part of the inner ear that turns sound into nerve signals.
Hair cell
The sensory cell of the ear. Bending its tiny hairs opens channels and changes its charge.
Tonotopy
An orderly map of sound frequency, found from the cochlea all the way to auditory cortex.
