How Neurons Talk

What it is. At a synapse, an electrical spike in one neuron triggers the release of a chemical messenger that changes the charge of the next neuron.

What it gives you. Synapses are where information is combined, filtered and stored. Most drugs that act on the brain work here.

Electrical in, chemical across, electrical out

Most synapses are chemical. A spike reaches the end of the axon, calcium enters and vesicles release transmitter into a narrow gap. Receptors on the far side respond within a fraction of a millisecond.

A small number of synapses are electrical. Here, channels called gap junctions connect two cells directly, and current flows across with no delay. They are useful where many cells must fire together.

Each neuron receives thousands of inputs. Some push it toward firing, some hold it back. The cell adds them together at the base of the axon, and only if the total crosses threshold does it send a spike.

The dream that settled it

  1. In the early 1900s, it was unclear whether synapses were electrical or chemical.
  2. Otto Loewi woke one night in 1921 with an experiment in mind, wrote it down and could not read his notes in the morning.
  3. The idea returned the next night. He stimulated the vagus nerve of a frog heart, which slowed it.
  4. He moved fluid from that heart to a second heart, which slowed as well.
  5. The nerve had released a chemical, later identified as acetylcholine. He won the Nobel Prize in 1936.

One clear experiment ended decades of debate.

Electrical and chemical synapses

  • Electrical Gap junctions join two cells so current flows directly. Fast, two-way and common in early development.
  • Chemical Transmitter crosses a gap and binds receptors. Slower but flexible, and able to amplify, invert or modify a signal.
  • Where they sit Axon to dendrite is most common, but synapses also form on cell bodies, spines and other axon terminals.

Across the gap, step by step

  • Synthesis Transmitters are made in the terminal or carried there from the cell body.
  • Storage Vesicles collect transmitter and dock near release sites on the terminal membrane.
  • Calcium entry The spike opens voltage-gated calcium channels. Calcium is the trigger for release.
  • Fusion SNARE proteins pull the vesicle into the membrane and its contents spill into the gap.
  • Binding Transmitter binds receptors on the target cell, opening channels or activating G-proteins.
  • Clearance Enzymes, reuptake pumps and diffusion end the signal so the synapse can respond again.

What the receiving cell does

  • EPSP An excitatory input lets positive ions in, nudging the cell toward threshold.
  • IPSP An inhibitory input lets chloride in or potassium out, moving the cell away from threshold.
  • Spatial summation Inputs arriving at the same moment on different dendrites add together.
  • Temporal summation Inputs arriving in quick succession at one synapse build on each other.
  • Shunting Inhibition near the cell body can cancel excitation flowing in from distant dendrites.
  • Modulation Some transmitters act slowly through chemical cascades, adjusting how a cell responds for minutes.

How one neuron decides

  1. Thousands of synapses deliver small inputs, each worth a fraction of a millivolt.
  2. Charge spreads along dendrites and fades with distance.
  3. At the axon hillock, all incoming changes are summed.
  4. If the total reaches threshold, a spike fires down the axon.
  5. Inhibitory inputs placed close to the hillock carry the most weight.

Position on the cell matters as much as strength.

Drugs and poisons at the synapse

  • Botulinum toxin Blocks acetylcholine release at muscles. Used in small doses to relax wrinkles and spasms.
  • Black widow venom Triggers massive release of acetylcholine, causing cramps and spasms.
  • Curare Blocks acetylcholine receptors on muscle. Once used on arrow tips, later in surgery.
  • Nerve gas Blocks the enzyme that clears acetylcholine, so muscles cannot relax.
  • Agonists Drugs that mimic a transmitter and activate its receptors.
  • Antagonists Drugs that block a receptor without activating it.

A rare startle condition described in nineteenth-century Maine lumberjacks, the Jumping Frenchmen, involves an extreme reaction to sudden sounds. A similar disorder, hyperekplexia, is caused by faulty glycine receptors, which normally provide inhibition in the spinal cord.

Safety. Breathe gently and stop if you feel dizzy.

Steps

  1. Picture a spike arriving at an axon terminal.
  2. Let the charge open calcium channels in the terminal.
  3. Calcium enters and triggers vesicles to fuse with the membrane and release transmitter.
  4. Follow the transmitter across a gap of about 20 to 50 nanometres.
  5. It binds receptors on the next cell, opening channels or starting chemical cascades.
  6. Now remove the transmitter.
  7. It is broken down by enzymes or taken back up into cells, which ends the signal in milliseconds.

Time. 3 minutes

Words to know

Vesicle
A small membrane sphere inside the axon terminal that stores transmitter molecules.
EPSP and IPSP
Small shifts in charge that make a neuron more likely (excitatory) or less likely (inhibitory) to fire.
Integration
The way a neuron adds up thousands of inputs across space and time to decide whether to fire.
