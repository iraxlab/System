Movement II

What it is. The brain controls movement in three levels: strategy in the association cortex and basal ganglia, tactics in motor cortex and cerebellum, and execution in the brainstem and spinal cord.

What it gives you. This hierarchy explains movement disorders such as Parkinson's and Huntington's disease, and why practice turns effort into automatic skill.

A hierarchy of control

A baseball pitcher shows all three levels. Choosing a pitch is strategy. Planning the arm's path and timing is tactics. Contracting the right muscles in the right order is execution. Each level uses sensory feedback to stay on target.

Commands travel from the brain to the spinal cord in two main groups of tracts. The lateral pathways, led by the corticospinal tract, control voluntary movement of the limbs. The ventromedial pathways control posture and balance.

Two large loops refine every movement. The basal ganglia help choose which action to start. The cerebellum compares intended movement with actual movement and corrects the difference.

From brain to spinal cord

  • Corticospinal tract About one million axons from motor cortex, crossing at the base of the brainstem. It controls fine finger movement.
  • Rubrospinal tract A smaller pathway from the midbrain that also controls the limbs.
  • Ventromedial tracts From the brainstem: vestibular, tectal and reticular tracts that maintain posture and orient the head.

Damage to the corticospinal tract often causes the Babinski sign, an upward toe response to stroking the sole.

Cortex before movement

  • Posterior parietal Combines vision, touch and body position to build a map of where things are relative to the body.
  • Prefrontal cortex Holds goals and selects actions. Damage leaves people unable to plan or follow through.
  • Premotor areas Neurons fire up to a second before movement, signalling intention rather than muscle activity.
  • Primary motor cortex Holds a body map. Hands and face take up far more area than the trunk.
  • Population coding Each M1 neuron prefers one direction. Direction is read from the combined vote of many cells.
  • Flexible map The motor map reorganises after injury or practice, expanding areas in heavy use.

Selecting what to do

  1. Cortex sends input to the striatum, made of the caudate and putamen.
  2. Output passes through the globus pallidus to the thalamus and back to the cortex.
  3. At rest, this loop holds movement back. Activation releases the chosen action.
  4. In Parkinson's disease, loss of dopamine cells leaves the brake on: slow, stiff movement and tremor.
  5. In Huntington's disease, loss of striatal cells removes the brake: involuntary, writhing movements.

L-dopa and deep brain stimulation both act on this loop.

Timing and correction

  • Structure Contains about half of all neurons in the brain, arranged in a highly regular circuit.
  • Role Compares the command sent with the movement made and adjusts the next one. Essential for smooth, accurate action.
  • Damage Causes ataxia: clumsy, poorly timed movements, overshooting targets and a broad, unsteady walk.

Alcohol affects the cerebellum early, which is why roadside sobriety tests check balance and coordination.

Brain control of movement

  • 1 million axons in each corticospinal tract
  • 50% of brain neurons are in the cerebellum
  • 80% of dopamine cells lost before Parkinson's symptoms appear
  • 3 levels of control: strategy, tactics, execution
  • 1 second before movement, premotor cells start firing
  • 2006 a paralysed man moves a cursor by thought through an implant

Brain-machine interfaces read the combined activity of motor cortex neurons. People with paralysis have used implanted electrode arrays to move cursors, robotic arms and their own stimulated muscles.

Safety. Move your head gently and stop if you feel dizzy.

Steps

  1. Reach for a cup on the table.
  2. Notice the decision to reach.
  3. Strategy: frontal and parietal cortex select the goal and the plan.
  4. Notice the path your arm takes.
  5. Tactics: motor cortex and cerebellum set the sequence and timing of muscle contractions.
  6. Notice your grip adjust as you lift.
  7. Execution: brainstem and spinal cord carry out the plan and correct it using feedback.

Time. 2 minutes

Words to know

Basal ganglia
Deep forebrain nuclei that help select and start wanted movements and suppress unwanted ones.
M1
Primary motor cortex, in front of the central sulcus. It holds a map of the body.
Cerebellum
The structure at the back of the brain that coordinates timing and corrects errors in movement.
