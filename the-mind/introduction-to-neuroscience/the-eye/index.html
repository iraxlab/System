The Eye

What it is. The eye focuses light onto the retina, a thin sheet of neural tissue where photoreceptors turn light into electrical signals and ganglion cells send the result to the brain.

What it gives you. Vision supplies most of what we know about the world around us. The retina also shows, in miniature, how every sensory system filters and simplifies its input.

A camera that edits its own image

The cornea and lens bend light so that it lands in focus on the retina at the back of the eye. The cornea does most of the bending. The lens adjusts for distance by changing shape, a process called accommodation.

The retina is built in an unexpected order. Light passes through layers of neurons before reaching the photoreceptors at the back. Signals then travel forward again, from photoreceptors to bipolar cells to ganglion cells, whose axons form the optic nerve.

About 125 million photoreceptors feed only about one million ganglion cells. The retina therefore compresses the image heavily, reporting edges and contrast rather than absolute brightness.

How the eye focuses

  • Cornea The clear front surface. It provides about two thirds of the eye's focusing power.
  • Lens Changes shape to focus near or far. It stiffens with age, which is why reading glasses become necessary.
  • Pupil Opens and closes to control light. Its size also changes depth of field, like a camera aperture.
  • Myopia Short-sightedness: the eye is too long, so distant images focus in front of the retina.
  • Hyperopia Long-sightedness: the eye is too short, so near objects focus behind the retina.
  • Pupillary reflex Light in one eye constricts both pupils. Doctors use it to check brainstem function.

Two systems for two kinds of light

  • Rods About 90 million per eye. Highly sensitive and used in dim light. They do not distinguish colour.
  • Cones About 5 million per eye, concentrated in the fovea. They work in daylight and give sharp, colour vision.
  • Three cone types Each responds best to blue, green or red wavelengths. Colour is computed from the ratio of their signals.

Most colour blindness comes from a missing or altered red or green cone pigment, and is far more common in men.

How light becomes a signal

  1. In the dark, sodium channels in the photoreceptor stay open. The cell is relatively charged and releases glutamate steadily.
  2. Light changes the shape of the pigment rhodopsin.
  3. This activates a G-protein cascade that lowers the level of cyclic GMP.
  4. Sodium channels close and the photoreceptor becomes more negative.
  5. Glutamate release falls. In the visual system, light reduces transmitter output.

One activated pigment molecule can close hundreds of channels, which makes rods sensitive to single photons.

What the ganglion cells report

  • ON and OFF cells ON-centre cells fire when light falls on the centre of their field; OFF-centre cells fire when it is removed.
  • Centre and surround The surround has the opposite effect to the centre, so cells respond best to contrast, not even light.
  • M and P types Large M-type cells signal motion and coarse shape. Smaller P-type cells signal fine detail and colour.

The retina

  • 125 million photoreceptors in each retina
  • 1 million ganglion cell axons in each optic nerve
  • 0.2 mm thickness of the human retina
  • 1 photon enough to change a rod's activity
  • 5 layers of cells: photoreceptor, horizontal, bipolar, amacrine, ganglion
  • 20-30 min for full dark adaptation

Some ganglion cells contain their own light pigment, melanopsin. They do not help with seeing; they tell the brain's daily clock whether it is day or night, and work even in some people who are otherwise blind.

Steps

  1. Close your left eye and look at a single point on a wall with your right.
  2. Hold a pen at arm's length to the right of that point and move it slowly outward.
  3. At about 15 degrees, the tip disappears. This is the blind spot, where the optic nerve leaves the eye.
  4. Go into a dark room and wait.
  5. Over 20 to 30 minutes, rod photoreceptors slowly regain sensitivity. This is dark adaptation.
  6. Look directly at a dim star at night, then just beside it.
  7. It appears brighter off-centre, because the centre of the retina has cones only and no rods.

Time. 15 minutes

Words to know

Fovea
A small pit at the centre of the retina with tightly packed cones, giving the sharpest vision.
Phototransduction
The chain of chemical steps by which a photoreceptor converts light into a change in charge.
Receptive field
The area of the visual field where light changes a given cell's firing.
