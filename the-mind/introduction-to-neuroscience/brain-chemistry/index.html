Brain Chemistry

What it is. Beyond fast point-to-point signalling, the brain uses three slower systems to set its overall state: hormones from the hypothalamus, the autonomic nervous system and diffuse modulatory systems that bathe whole regions in transmitters.

What it gives you. These systems govern stress, arousal, mood and sleep. They are the main targets of psychiatric medication and of most recreational drugs.

Setting the state of the whole brain

Most synapses are narrow and fast, linking one neuron to a few others. But some signals need to reach many targets at once and last longer. The brain uses three systems for this, each slower and broader than the last.

The hypothalamus, a structure the size of a grape, controls the pituitary gland and through it most of the body's hormones. It also runs the autonomic nervous system, which adjusts heart rate, digestion and blood flow.

Finally, a few thousand neurons in the brainstem and forebrain send axons across the entire brain. They release norepinephrine, serotonin, dopamine and acetylcholine, setting levels of alertness, mood and motivation.

Command centre for hormones

  • Posterior pituitary Neurons release oxytocin and vasopressin directly into the blood. Vasopressin controls water balance.
  • Anterior pituitary Hypothalamic releasing hormones travel through local blood vessels and trigger pituitary hormones.
  • Stress axis The hypothalamus, pituitary and adrenal glands together release cortisol, which mobilises fuel.
  • Feedback Cortisol acts back on the brain to switch off the stress response once the threat passes.
  • Chronic stress Sustained high cortisol can damage hippocampal neurons and impair memory.
  • Homeostasis Monitors temperature, blood sugar and salt, then corrects them through hormones and behaviour.

Three divisions

  • Sympathetic Prepares the body for action: raises heart rate, widens pupils and airways, slows digestion. Uses norepinephrine at its targets.
  • Parasympathetic Supports rest and digestion: slows the heart, stimulates digestion and saliva. Uses acetylcholine at its targets.
  • Enteric A network of about 500 million neurons in the gut wall that runs digestion largely on its own.

Four groups that shape the whole brain

  1. Norepinephrine: the locus coeruleus, about 12,000 neurons per side, raises alertness to new and important events.
  2. Serotonin: the raphe nuclei regulate sleep and waking, mood and some aspects of emotional behaviour.
  3. Dopamine: the substantia nigra supports movement; the ventral tegmental area supports reward and motivation.
  4. Acetylcholine: the basal forebrain supports attention, learning and memory.
  5. Each group is small, but a single neuron can contact over 250,000 others.

Loss of basal forebrain acetylcholine neurons is one of the earliest changes in Alzheimer's disease.

How mind-altering substances work

  • Hallucinogens LSD and psilocybin act on serotonin receptors in the cortex, altering perception and thought.
  • Stimulants Cocaine and amphetamine increase dopamine and norepinephrine, raising alertness and confidence.
  • Dependence Repeated use alters the systems involved, so normal function comes to require the drug.

Chemical control

  • 4 g approximate weight of the hypothalamus
  • 12,000 neurons in each locus coeruleus
  • 250,000+ synapses from one locus coeruleus neuron
  • 500 million neurons in the gut's enteric system
  • 2000 Nobel Prize to Carlsson for dopamine
  • 3 divisions of the autonomic system

Serotonin is made from tryptophan, an amino acid from food. Diets very low in tryptophan can measurably lower brain serotonin within hours, and in vulnerable people briefly worsen mood.

Safety. Stand up slowly if you feel light-headed.

Steps

  1. Recall a moment of sudden alarm, such as a near-miss on the road.
  2. Notice the racing heart and dry mouth.
  3. The sympathetic nervous system acts within seconds, releasing norepinephrine to heart and vessels.
  4. Notice that the tension lasts for minutes.
  5. The hypothalamus has triggered cortisol release through the pituitary and adrenal glands.
  6. Notice your attention narrowing.
  7. The locus coeruleus has raised norepinephrine across the cortex, sharpening vigilance.

Time. 3 minutes

Words to know

Homeostasis
The regulation of internal conditions, such as temperature and blood chemistry, within a narrow range.
Autonomic nervous system
The part of the nervous system that controls organs, glands and blood vessels without conscious effort.
Diffuse modulatory system
A small group of neurons with widely branching axons that adjusts the activity of large brain regions.
