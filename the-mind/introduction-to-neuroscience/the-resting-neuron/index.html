The Resting Neuron

What it is. At rest, the inside of a neuron sits about 65 millivolts more negative than the outside. That stored charge comes from uneven ion concentrations and a membrane that lets potassium leak out.

What it gives you. This resting charge is the battery that powers every signal. Without it, no neuron can fire.

Why a cell holds a charge

A neuron is not a copper wire. Its interior is a salty fluid with poor conductivity, and the membrane leaks. To move signals over distance, it relies on stored electrical charge across its membrane.

Two things create that charge. Pumps keep potassium concentrated inside the cell and sodium outside. Then channels that are selective for potassium let it leak out, carrying positive charge with it.

As positive charge leaves, the inside turns negative and begins to pull potassium back. The resting value is where those two forces balance, adjusted slightly by a small leak of sodium.

Why a neuron cannot work like a cable

  • Poor conductor The fluid inside an axon resists current far more than metal. A passive signal fades within a few millimetres.
  • Leaky insulation The membrane lets charge escape along its length, like a garden hose with holes.
  • The solution A stored charge that can be released at every point along the axon, renewing the signal as it travels.

Fluid, membrane and proteins

  • Water The solvent. Its slightly charged molecules surround ions and keep them dissolved.
  • Ions Sodium, potassium, calcium and chloride carry charge through the fluid on both sides.
  • Lipid bilayer The membrane's double layer of fat blocks ions completely. Only proteins can let them through.
  • Ion channels Protein pores that open for specific ions. Potassium channels pass potassium but not the smaller sodium.
  • Ion pumps Proteins that spend ATP to move ions against their gradients and build up concentration differences.
  • Charge separation A thin layer of charge sits on each side of the membrane. The bulk fluid stays neutral.

What moves an ion

  • Diffusion Ions move from high to low concentration, provided a channel is open for them.
  • Electrical force Opposite charges attract and like charges repel, so the membrane voltage pushes or pulls on each ion.
  • Balance For each ion there is one voltage where the two forces cancel. This is described by the Nernst equation.

How the resting charge forms

  1. The pump moves three sodium ions out and two potassium ions in for every ATP it spends.
  2. Potassium is now about twenty times more concentrated inside than outside.
  3. Potassium leak channels let it flow outward, carrying positive charge out of the cell.
  4. The inside becomes negative and starts to hold potassium back.
  5. At about minus 65 millivolts, outward push and inward pull reach a steady state.

The membrane at rest is about forty times more permeable to potassium than to sodium.

The resting neuron

  • -65 mV typical resting charge inside a neuron
  • -80 mV equilibrium voltage for potassium
  • +62 mV equilibrium voltage for sodium
  • 20:1 potassium concentration, inside versus outside
  • 70% of the brain's ATP may go to running ion pumps
  • 3:2 sodium out to potassium in, per pump cycle

Why the brain controls it so tightly

  1. The resting charge depends most on the potassium level outside the cell.
  2. A rise in outside potassium makes neurons less negative and easier to fire.
  3. The blood-brain barrier keeps blood potassium swings from reaching brain tissue.
  4. Astrocytes absorb excess potassium and spread it through their linked network.
  5. Lethal injection uses potassium chloride because it stops the heart by collapsing this charge.

Small changes here have large effects on how excitable the whole brain is.

The first potassium channel gene was found in a fruit fly mutant that shakes its legs under anaesthesia. The gene was named Shaker, and the channel family that followed shares the name.

Steps

  1. Picture a membrane with potassium inside and sodium outside.
  2. Open only potassium channels in your mind.
  3. Potassium flows out down its concentration gradient, leaving the inside negative.
  4. Let the negative charge build until it pulls potassium back as hard as the gradient pushes it out.
  5. That balance point is the equilibrium voltage for potassium, about minus 80 millivolts.
  6. Add a small sodium leak.
  7. The resting value settles slightly less negative, about minus 65. The sodium-potassium pump keeps the gradients in place.

Time. 10 minutes

Words to know

Ion
An atom with an electrical charge. Sodium and potassium are positive; chloride is negative.
Equilibrium voltage
The charge at which an ion's electrical pull exactly balances its concentration push, so no net flow occurs.
Sodium-potassium pump
A membrane protein that uses ATP to push sodium out and pull potassium in, three for two.
