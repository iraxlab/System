Motivation

What it is. Motivation is the drive that turns needs into action. The hypothalamus monitors body states such as fuel and temperature, and dopamine circuits assign value to the actions that satisfy them.

What it gives you. Understanding motivation explains hunger and fullness, body weight regulation and why addictive drugs take over behaviour.

Why we act

Behaviour has a direction. Some drives are concrete, such as hunger, thirst or cold. Others are abstract, such as the urge to finish a project. In each case, the brain detects a gap between how things are and how they should be, and pushes behaviour to close it.

The hypothalamus is the main sensor for body needs. It tracks stored fuel, blood sugar, salt and temperature. When values drift, it triggers three responses: hormones, autonomic changes and motivated behaviour.

Dopamine circuits add value. They mark which actions and cues lead to reward, so that we pursue them again. The same circuits can be hijacked by drugs, which produce strong wanting without meeting any real need.

How the brain tracks stored fuel

  • Leptin Fat cells release leptin in proportion to their size. High leptin reduces appetite and raises metabolic rate.
  • Low leptin When fat stores fall, leptin drops. The hypothalamus increases hunger and slows metabolism to conserve fuel.
  • Lesion studies Damage to one part of the hypothalamus causes overeating; damage to another causes refusal to eat.

Mice lacking the leptin gene grow to three times normal weight. Leptin injections reverse this in them, but rarely help human obesity.

Why a meal starts and stops

  1. Ghrelin from the empty stomach rises before meals and stimulates hunger.
  2. Seeing and smelling food triggers saliva, insulin and stomach acid in advance.
  3. Stretch of the stomach sends fullness signals through the vagus nerve.
  4. CCK released from the gut by fatty food signals satiety.
  5. Insulin from the pancreas rises as glucose is absorbed and reduces further eating.

These signals end a meal. Leptin sets how much is eaten over weeks.

The role of dopamine

  • Self-stimulation Rats press a lever thousands of times an hour to stimulate dopamine pathways in their brain.
  • Wanting, not liking Dopamine drives the pursuit of a reward. Pleasure itself depends more on opioid and other systems.
  • Prediction Dopamine cells fire most to unexpected reward and dip when an expected reward fails to arrive.
  • Addiction Drugs raise dopamine directly and powerfully, building strong wanting that persists even as pleasure fades.
  • Serotonin and mood Carbohydrate-rich food raises brain serotonin, which helps explain cravings during low mood.
  • Temperature The hypothalamus triggers shivering, sweating and behaviour such as finding shelter to hold body heat near 37 degrees.

Motivation

  • 1994 leptin discovered
  • 37°C core body temperature set point
  • 3x weight of mice lacking leptin
  • 20 min roughly, for fullness signals to build after eating
  • 1954 brain self-stimulation first described in rats
  • 2 components of reward: wanting and liking

In the 1950s and 60s, a few patients with implanted electrodes were allowed to stimulate their own brains. Some pressed the button repeatedly, describing a pleasant urge to continue rather than clear pleasure.

Steps

  1. Before a meal, notice your hunger level on a scale of one to ten.
  2. Eat slowly and check again halfway through.
  3. Stretch receptors in the stomach and gut hormones such as CCK have begun signalling fullness.
  4. Wait twenty minutes after finishing.
  5. Fullness grows as insulin and other signals reach the hypothalamus.
  6. Notice whether you still want dessert.
  7. Wanting is driven partly by dopamine and can continue after the body's need is met.

Time. One meal

Words to know

Leptin
A hormone released by fat cells that tells the brain how much fuel is stored.
Satiety
The feeling of fullness that ends a meal.
Wanting and liking
Two components of reward: the drive toward something (dopamine) and the pleasure it gives (opioids).
