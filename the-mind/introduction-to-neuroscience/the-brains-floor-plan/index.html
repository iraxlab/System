The Brain's Floor Plan

What it is. The nervous system develops from a simple tube in the embryo. Its regions, from spinal cord to cortex, follow from how that tube swells and folds.

What it gives you. A clear map of the regions and directions makes every later topic easier to place and remember.

One tube, three swellings

Three weeks after conception, a flat sheet of cells on the embryo's back rolls into a tube. Its front end swells into three chambers: forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain. The rest becomes the spinal cord.

The forebrain later divides into the cerebral hemispheres and the diencephalon, which holds the thalamus and hypothalamus. The midbrain stays small. The hindbrain forms the cerebellum, pons and medulla.

In humans the hemispheres grow so large that they fold over everything else. Folding increases surface area, and most of that surface is neocortex, the six-layered tissue that handles perception, language and planning.

From sheet to brain

  1. A flat neural plate forms on the back of the embryo in the third week.
  2. Its edges rise and fuse to form the neural tube, closing by the fourth week.
  3. The front end swells into forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain.
  4. The hemispheres grow outward and backward, folding as they expand.
  5. Failure to close causes spina bifida or anencephaly. Folic acid before pregnancy lowers the risk.

The fluid space inside the tube becomes the ventricles.

Major regions of the adult brain

  • Cerebrum The two hemispheres. Cortex on the surface, white matter and deep nuclei below.
  • Thalamus The relay station that passes nearly all sensory input to the cortex.
  • Hypothalamus Controls hormones, body temperature, hunger, thirst and daily rhythms.
  • Midbrain Contains centres for eye movements, hearing reflexes and dopamine cells.
  • Cerebellum Coordinates movement and timing. Contains more neurons than the rest of the brain combined.
  • Pons and medulla Carry tracts between brain and body and control breathing and heart rate.

The language of direction

  • Rostral and caudal Toward the nose and toward the tail. In the human brain, front and back.
  • Dorsal and ventral Toward the back and toward the belly. In the brain, top and bottom.
  • Medial and lateral Toward the midline and toward the side. Ipsilateral means same side, contralateral opposite.

How the brain is guarded

  1. The skull forms a rigid outer shell.
  2. The dura mater is a tough leather-like layer just inside the skull.
  3. The arachnoid and pia enclose a space filled with cerebrospinal fluid.
  4. The fluid cushions the brain and reduces its effective weight from 1,400 g to about 50 g.
  5. The blood-brain barrier filters what passes from blood into brain tissue.

The ventricles produce about half a litre of fluid every day.

Seeing the living brain

  • MRI Magnetic fields and radio waves produce detailed images of structure without radiation.
  • PET Tracks radioactive tracers to show where the brain uses glucose or blood flow increases.
  • fMRI Measures changes in blood oxygen to show which regions are active during a task.

CT scanning uses X-rays from many angles and is still the fastest scan for a suspected stroke.

What sets our brain apart

  • 1,400 g average adult brain weight
  • 2% of body weight, using about 20% of its oxygen
  • 2,500 cm² surface area of the unfolded cortex
  • 6 layers in the neocortex
  • 31 pairs of spinal nerves
  • 12 pairs of cranial nerves

The retina is part of the brain. It forms as an outgrowth of the embryonic forebrain, which is why an eye exam is the only way to see central nervous tissue without surgery.

Steps

  1. Place one hand flat on top of your head, facing forward.
  2. Move it toward the forehead.
  3. That is rostral, toward the nose. The back of the head is caudal.
  4. Move it to the top of the head.
  5. That is dorsal. The underside of the brain is ventral.
  6. Point to the middle line between your eyes.
  7. That is medial. Toward the ears is lateral. These terms locate any structure in the brain.

Time. 3 minutes

Words to know

Neural tube
The hollow tube in the early embryo that becomes the brain and spinal cord.
Ventricles
Connected chambers inside the brain filled with cerebrospinal fluid.
Meninges
Three protective membranes around the brain: dura, arachnoid and pia.
