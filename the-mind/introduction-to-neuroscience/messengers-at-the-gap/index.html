Messengers at the Gap

What it is. Neurotransmitters are the chemical messengers released at synapses. The same transmitter can excite one cell and inhibit another, depending on the receptor it meets.

What it gives you. Understanding transmitters and their receptors explains how medicines, caffeine, alcohol and recreational drugs change thought and mood.

Three families of messenger

Amino acids such as glutamate, GABA and glycine handle most fast signalling. Glutamate is the main excitatory transmitter in the brain; GABA is the main inhibitory one.

Amines, including acetylcholine, dopamine, norepinephrine, serotonin and histamine, come from smaller groups of cells and often adjust the state of whole regions. Peptides, longer chains of amino acids, act more slowly and are released under heavy activity.

The receptor decides the effect. Acetylcholine excites skeletal muscle through one receptor type and slows the heart through another. One messenger, many meanings.

Main transmitters and their roles

  • Glutamate The main excitatory transmitter in the brain. Essential for learning, and toxic in excess.
  • GABA The main inhibitory transmitter. Calming drugs such as benzodiazepines boost its effect.
  • Acetylcholine Drives skeletal muscle and supports attention and memory in the brain.
  • Dopamine Involved in movement, motivation and reward. Lost in Parkinson's disease.
  • Serotonin Shapes mood, sleep and appetite. Target of the most common antidepressants.
  • Peptides Include endorphins and substance P. They modulate pain, stress and appetite.

How a chemical is proven to be a transmitter

  • Location It must be made and stored in the presynaptic neuron.
  • Release It must be released when the axon terminal is stimulated.
  • Mimicry Applying it directly must reproduce the effect of natural release on the target cell.

Fast locks and slow locks

  • Ionotropic A channel with a binding site. Glutamate AMPA and NMDA receptors and GABA-A receptors work this way.
  • Metabotropic Activates G-proteins inside the cell, which open channels or start chemical cascades.
  • Amplification One activated receptor can trigger thousands of second-messenger molecules, multiplying the signal.

What common substances do at the synapse

  1. Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors. Adenosine builds during waking and promotes sleep.
  2. Nicotine activates one type of acetylcholine receptor, including in reward circuits.
  3. Alcohol enhances GABA receptors and dampens glutamate receptors.
  4. Cocaine blocks reuptake of dopamine and norepinephrine, extending their action.
  5. SSRIs block reuptake of serotonin. Their mood effects take weeks to build.

Each drug works by borrowing or blocking a system that already exists.

Opiates and cannabis

  1. Morphine binds opioid receptors, which the brain already uses for its own endorphins.
  2. Endorphins are released under stress and pain and reduce the perception of pain.
  3. Cannabis acts on cannabinoid receptors, among the most common receptors in the brain.
  4. The brain makes its own cannabinoids, such as anandamide, on demand.
  5. These travel backward across the synapse and reduce transmitter release.

Finding a drug receptor usually means finding a natural messenger.

Messengers

  • 100+ known neurotransmitters and neuropeptides
  • 5 dopamine receptor types
  • 14+ serotonin receptor types
  • 1 ms response time of fast ionotropic receptors
  • seconds to minutes span of metabotropic effects
  • 1921 acetylcholine shown to act at a synapse

Most of the body's serotonin is not in the brain. It is made in the gut and carried in blood platelets, where it helps blood vessels constrict at a wound.

Safety. Read only. Never change a medicine without your doctor.

Steps

  1. Pick one transmitter, such as dopamine.
  2. Ask where it is made and which cells release it.
  3. Dopamine comes from small groups of cells in the midbrain that send axons widely.
  4. Ask which receptors it meets.
  5. Dopamine has at least five receptor types, all acting through G-proteins.
  6. Ask how the signal ends.
  7. A reuptake pump returns it to the terminal. Cocaine blocks this pump, so dopamine lingers.

Time. 5 minutes

Words to know

Ionotropic receptor
A receptor that is itself a channel. It opens within a millisecond of binding transmitter.
Metabotropic receptor
A receptor that acts through G-proteins and second messengers. Slower, longer lasting and able to amplify.
Second messenger
A small molecule inside the cell, such as cyclic AMP, that carries a receptor's signal to its targets.
