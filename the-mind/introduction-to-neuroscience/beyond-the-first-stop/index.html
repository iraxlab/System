Beyond the First Stop

What it is. Beyond primary visual cortex, two streams carry visual information onward: a dorsal stream toward the parietal lobe for motion and action, and a ventral stream toward the temporal lobe for recognising objects and faces.

What it gives you. These streams explain why some brain injuries remove the ability to see motion, colour or faces while leaving the rest of vision intact.

Where and what

Beyond V1 lie more than two dozen visual areas, each with its own map of the visual field. They are not arranged in a single line. Two broad streams stand out, each fed mostly by different channels from V1.

The dorsal stream runs up toward the parietal lobe. It extends the magno pathway and specialises in motion, spatial position and guiding action. The ventral stream runs down toward the temporal lobe and extends the parvo and blob pathways, handling shape, colour and identity.

Perception does not depend on a single final cell. It emerges from many broadly tuned areas working in parallel, closer to an orchestra than an assembly line.

Motion and action

  • Area MT Almost all cells respond to motion in a specific direction, arranged in direction columns like orientation columns in V1.
  • Area MST Cells respond to complex motion: expanding, contracting or rotating patterns, useful when moving through space.
  • Causal evidence Weak stimulation of an MT column in monkeys shifts which direction of motion they report seeing.

One patient's experience

  1. In 1983, a woman in Munich had a stroke that damaged motion areas on both sides of her brain.
  2. Her vision for colour, shape and depth remained largely normal.
  3. Pouring coffee, she saw it frozen in the cup, then suddenly overflowing.
  4. Crossing a road, cars seemed far away, then suddenly next to her.
  5. The condition, akinetopsia, shows that motion has its own dedicated processing.

Motion is not inferred from snapshots. The brain computes it directly.

Shape, colour and identity

  • Area V4 Cells are selective for both colour and shape. Damage nearby can cause achromatopsia, a loss of colour vision with healthy cones.
  • Area IT Cells respond to complex shapes and objects. It is also important for visual memory.
  • Face cells A small share of IT cells respond strongly to faces. In humans, a matching region lights up on fMRI.

Is one cell enough to recognise a person?

  • The idea Perhaps a handful of neurons fire only to one specific person, such as your grandmother.
  • No evidence Recordings across the monkey brain show no area with cells for each of the millions of objects we know.
  • Broad tuning Every level of the visual system uses cells that respond to a range of inputs, not one.
  • Many properties A cell tuned to one feature also responds to others, such as size and motion.
  • Fragility If a memory relied on five cells, a small injury could erase a person entirely.
  • The alternative Recognition likely depends on patterns of activity spread across large groups of cells.

Beyond V1

  • 24+ visual areas beyond primary visual cortex
  • 2 main streams: dorsal and ventral
  • 1 million ganglion cells feeding the system
  • 1 billion+ cortical neurons involved in vision
  • 1960 random-dot stereograms invented
  • 1983 first clear case of motion blindness reported

Random-dot stereograms show no visible shape to either eye alone. A square appears only when the brain fuses the two images, proving that depth can be computed before objects are recognised.

Steps

  1. Watch a person walk across the room.
  2. Notice that you see motion as one smooth event.
  3. Area MT in the dorsal stream computes direction and speed of movement.
  4. Look at a familiar face, then at a familiar object.
  5. Different regions in the ventral stream respond more strongly to each.
  6. Look at a coloured object in dim light.
  7. Colour fades as cones lose sensitivity, but shape remains. Colour and form use partly separate routes.

Time. 2 minutes

Words to know

Area MT
A region in the dorsal stream where nearly every cell responds to motion in a particular direction.
Area IT
Inferior temporal cortex: the end of the ventral stream, involved in object recognition and visual memory.
Prosopagnosia
An inability to recognise faces despite otherwise normal vision.
